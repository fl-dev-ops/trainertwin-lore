What they don’t tell you about leaving Google after 16 years to build your own company:

You have to become a beginner again.

Six months into building PitchNinjas, I was stuck. Our pricing model wasn’t working, and a client had asked me something I didn’t know how to answer.

I opened an email to a mentor. Then stared at the blinking cursor before closing it.

At Google, I had spent 16 years learning how to be the person with the answer. Promotions, panels, flying around the world to lead teams and facilitate programs.

Competence was currency, and I had become very good at trading in it.
What I hadn’t realised was that being known for having the answers had made me deeply uncomfortable admitting when I didn’t.

When I left to build something of my own, I thought the hard part would be strategy, sales or getting the financial model right.

It wasn’t.

The hard part was letting other people see me not know what I was doing.

I did eventually send that email. I rewrote it four times first, mostly to make it sound less like: “Hi, I have no idea what I’m doing.” But that was basically the truth.

I had to get comfortable saying three words I’d spent years avoiding:

I don’t know.

Expertise can make you confident but it can also make you guarded.

Asking for help doesn’t erase everything you know. It’s often how you earn the knowledge you need for whatever comes next.

I still hesitate before sending a message that admits I’m stuck.

Sixteen years of habits don’t disappear just because your job title changes.

Sometimes the hardest part of moving forward is giving yourself permission to be new at something again.
