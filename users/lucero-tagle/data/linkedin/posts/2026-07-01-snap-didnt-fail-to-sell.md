Snap didn’t fail to sell the vision. It failed to answer one question: “Why should I buy this today?”

On June 16th, Evan Spiegel unveiled Snap Specs at the Augmented World Expo. Stock went down 10%.

Why?

The customer wanted the present:
❓ What do these glasses actually do for me today?
❓ Why should I spend $2,195 on them?
❌ Snap didn't answer either question.

Evan Spiegel made a strong case for where computing might be heading. Investors could see the story. But they were also thinking about:
❓ $2,195 price tag
❓ Consumer demand uncertainty
❓ Meta and Google competition
❓ Years of heavy AR investment
❓ Pressure on Snap's advertising business
❓ Whether Specs could ever become a profitable product

Consumers had a more basic reaction: "What do I get for that money right now?"

That gap is where a lot of pitches fall apart.
The vision may be right. 
But people need a reason to care before they are asked to believe.

The iPhone launch is a useful contrast.

Steve Jobs didn't start with: 
"This will redefine mobile computing."
He showed people a phone, an iPod, and an internet communicator in one device.

He made the future feel useful on an ordinary day.
Then the bigger story made sense.

I see this all the time when coaching pitches at PitchNinjas:
✅ Start with the problem your audience already feels
✅ Show the value they can use now
✅ Then talk about the future you want to build

If you skip the first two, the vision has nowhere to land.

I don't think the market rejected Snap's vision outright.
I think it questioned the path from "interesting" to "worth buying."

That matters for founders, enterprise teams, and anyone trying to sell change inside an organisation.

People are much more willing to buy into tomorrow when today already makes sense.
