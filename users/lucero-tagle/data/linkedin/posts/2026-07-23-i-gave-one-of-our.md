I gave one of our junior members of the team feedback last year. Calm, measured. She nodded and left.

Two days later I found out what she actually heard.

"𝘠𝘰𝘶'𝘳𝘦 𝘯𝘰𝘵 𝘨𝘰𝘰𝘥 𝘦𝘯𝘰𝘶𝘨𝘩 𝘢𝘯𝘥 𝘦𝘷𝘦𝘳𝘺𝘰𝘯𝘦 𝘤𝘢𝘯 𝘴𝘦𝘦 𝘪𝘵."

I hadn't said anything close to that. But it didn't matter.

That moment made me think about my own communication style.
I call it the internal translation.

The gap between what leaves your mouth and what lands in someone else's head, filtered through their history, their fear, their morning.

Most managers we coach struggle because they've never been taught to check what's actually getting through.

It shows up like this.

A junior team member goes quiet after feedback.
↳ The manager reads it as disengagement, indifference, maybe attitude.
↳ 𝘞𝘦 𝘥𝘰𝘯'𝘵 𝘴𝘦𝘦 𝘵𝘩𝘦𝘮 𝘩𝘦𝘢𝘳𝘪𝘯𝘨: "𝘠𝘰𝘶'𝘳𝘦 𝘧𝘢𝘪𝘭𝘪𝘯𝘨. 𝘈𝘯𝘥 𝘦𝘷𝘦𝘳𝘺𝘰𝘯𝘦 𝘪𝘯 𝘵𝘩𝘪𝘴 𝘳𝘰𝘰𝘮 𝘬𝘯𝘰𝘸𝘴 𝘪𝘵."

An executive interrupts a presentation halfway through.
↳ The room assumes rudeness, impatience, disrespect.
↳ 𝘞𝘦 𝘥𝘰𝘯'𝘵 𝘴𝘦𝘦 𝘵𝘩𝘦𝘮 𝘵𝘩𝘪𝘯𝘬𝘪𝘯𝘨: "𝘐 𝘯𝘦𝘦𝘥 𝘰𝘯𝘦 𝘯𝘶𝘮𝘣𝘦𝘳 𝘧𝘰𝘳 𝘵𝘩𝘦 𝘣𝘰𝘢𝘳𝘥 𝘪𝘯 𝘵𝘸𝘦𝘯𝘵𝘺 𝘮𝘪𝘯𝘶𝘵𝘦𝘴. 𝘞𝘩𝘦𝘳𝘦 𝘪𝘴 𝘪𝘵?"

A client replies to your detailed proposal with two words.
↳ You read it as dismissal, coldness, a bad sign.
↳ 𝘞𝘦 𝘥𝘰𝘯'𝘵 𝘴𝘦𝘦 𝘵𝘩𝘦𝘮 𝘧𝘪𝘳𝘪𝘯𝘨 𝘰𝘧𝘧 𝘵𝘩𝘢𝘵 𝘳𝘦𝘱𝘭𝘺 𝘣𝘦𝘵𝘸𝘦𝘦𝘯 𝘵𝘩𝘦𝘪𝘳 𝘵𝘩𝘪𝘳𝘥 𝘢𝘯𝘥 𝘧𝘰𝘶𝘳𝘵𝘩 𝘣𝘢𝘤𝘬-𝘵𝘰-𝘣𝘢𝘤𝘬 𝘤𝘢𝘭𝘭 𝘰𝘧 𝘵𝘩𝘦 𝘮𝘰𝘳𝘯𝘪𝘯𝘨.

The response you're reacting to is almost never the full story.

Great communication isn't about choosing better words.

It's the discipline to check what someone is hearing before you react to how they responded.
