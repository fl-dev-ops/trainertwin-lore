A new salesperson can produce the work of a twenty-year veteran by Friday of their first week. That does not make them one.
Two developments landed last week. The British government confirmed a pilot that sends young people through a short AI bootcamp and straight into a paid Level 3 apprenticeship, with JD Sports, BAE Systems, PA Consulting and Agilisys among the employers. Meta launched Muse, an agent that opens a browser, fills in forms and negotiates on a person’s behalf, available to adults in the United States inside WhatsApp. One story is about how people learn a trade. The other is about how much work machines now finish without us.
Read them together and one question arrives for every sales leader. If AI performs the research, the writing and the preparation, where does a new salesperson learn to think? Judgement is built by working through material that resists you slightly. Remove that struggle and you get output without understanding. The new person looks productive for a fortnight, then cannot answer a single follow-up question in the room. This week in the salesxcellence newsletter I set out the four tests I apply before letting AI take over any task, six rules for protecting the apprenticeship, and a way to decide where AI belongs by tenure rather than by licence. Efficiency creates time. Apprenticeship turns that time into judgement.
What is the one task on your team that you would never let AI do first?
