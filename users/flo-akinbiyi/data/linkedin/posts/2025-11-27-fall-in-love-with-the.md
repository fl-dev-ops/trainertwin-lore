“Fall in love with the problem, not the solution.” 

Yesterday at the DOERS Summit I shared the stage with Uri Levine, founder of Waze and Moovit. A guy who didn’t just build one unicorn, he built two. 

And the man walks around wearing a shirt with one line printed on it: 

“Fall in love with the problem, not the solution.” 

Simple. Direct. Almost annoyingly true. 

This sentence stayed with me, on my way home. 

Because if I’m being honest, I have been in love with my craft. 

The workshops.
The coaching sessions.
The frameworks.
The delivery.
All the solutions.

But the real problem I should be obsessed with is much more uncomfortable: 

A lot of leaders have ideas worth listening to, but they communicate them so poorly that nobody pays attention. 

Not because they’re not brilliant.
Not because the ideas aren’t relevant.
But because the message doesn’t land.
The body language is off.
The voice lacks presence.
And the audience politely disconnects. 

And that has a cost: 
Opportunities slip away.
Someone less experienced speaks with confidence and gets the moment. 

That’s the part that really bothers me. 

Because I work with executives, government leaders, senior directors, people who have responsibility, vision, and influence. 
And too often their message doesn’t reflect their capability. 

So maybe this is my shift or maybe it’s just finally naming what’s been there all along: 

I don’t want to be in love with public speaking as an activity.
Or coaching as a product.
Or frameworks as the shiny packaging.
I want to be in love with the problem: 

Helping leaders make their ideas impossible to ignore. 
Because communication isn’t a “nice to have” 
It’s the skill that decides whether your ideas live or die. 

When communication works, things change. 

So thank you, Uri for the reminder. 

From now on, I’m not refining the solution. 

I am chasing the problem until it’s gone!
