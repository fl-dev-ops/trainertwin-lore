AI evals have changed a lot, even since the start of this year. A lot of the work can now be *thoughtfully* handed over to harnesses like Codex/Claude Code.

One of the hardest parts of building good AI products is building good evals. But there’s also so much noise around what good evals even mean.

I usually see people fall into two camps: 
⛳ The first camp makes evals extremely pedantic. Everything needs a process, a rubric, a taxonomy, a manual review step, and ten layers of discussion. Some of that is useful, but a lot of it falls apart the moment the product goes into production and real users start doing weird things.

⛳ The second camp treats evals like a checklist. Add a few LLM judges, create a dashboard, get some scores, and call it a day. It feels cool yep, but it doesn’t always tell you what to fix or how to make the product better and it ends up being a cost bomb.

We kept seeing this again and again while consulting with companies and working on real AI products. So we put together a tight three week program on how evals are actually getting built today called "Beyond Evals: Designing Improvement Flywheels for AI Products". 

We just wrapped up our first cohort last week and are super stoked with the feedback.

The overarching goal was not to give people another checklist. The goal was to teach the improvement loop (below):

How do you decide what to evaluate? How do you build useful datasets? When should you use human judgment? When can AI help? When should AI absolutely not help? How do you take failures and turn them into better product behavior? How do you design architecture-specific evals for RAG, tool calling agents, multi-turn, multi-agent systems, etc.? 

A big part of the course was also having students use coding agents to augment their eval workflows to make the boring and repetitive parts faster, so they could spend more time thinking about the actual product and incorporate their taste.

Students built eval systems for everything from simple tool calling agents to more complex multi turn and multi agent workflows. And seeing people connect the dots across product, data, engineering, and judgment was incredibly cool.
