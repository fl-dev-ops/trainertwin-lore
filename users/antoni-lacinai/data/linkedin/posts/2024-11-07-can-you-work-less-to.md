Can you work less to get more?
Yes! and No...

Half of all employees prefer a 𝗳𝗼𝘂𝗿-𝗱𝗮𝘆 𝘄𝗼𝗿𝗸𝘄𝗲𝗲𝗸 over six-hour workdays — with a maintained salary. 

But 7 out of 10 employers don’t want any work-hour reductions at all, claiming severe productivity losses...

 𝗜𝘀 𝘁𝗵𝗶𝘀 𝘁𝗿𝘂𝗲? 𝗧𝗵𝗲 𝗮𝗻𝘀𝘄𝗲𝗿 𝗶𝘀 𝗬𝗲𝘀. 𝗔𝗻𝗱 𝗡𝗼. 

-	𝗬𝗲𝘀, 𝘆𝗼𝘂’𝗿𝗲 𝗿𝗶𝗴𝗵𝘁: There are simple jobs that just need to get done. It takes the time it takes. For those working these jobs, shorter workdays won’t increase productivity at all. It might even decrease it, and your employees could become burned out. There’s also a risk that people in the healthcare sector, or within customer service could run into problems if the client or patient suffers when availability drops. On the other hand, healthcare staff are exhausted and likely need more colleagues to support one another — and perhaps shorter work hours. 

-	𝗡𝗼, 𝘆𝗼𝘂’𝗿𝗲 𝘄𝗿𝗼𝗻𝗴: Among those with more complex jobs and for many desk employees, productivity is maintained, or even increased, with shorter work hours. Studies show that this is particularly well-suited in industries where knowledge work and creative problem-solving are the focus, such as tech, research, marketing, and consulting. Here, success is measured not just in hours worked but in results and innovation. Roles like IT specialists, project managers, and creative professionals can benefit greatly from the extra recovery a shorter workweek provides. 

But how can that be? The simple answer is 𝗥𝗲𝗰𝗼𝘃𝗲𝗿𝘆. You wouldn’t want a doctor who’s been awake for 48 hours or a nurse who hasn’t had proper rest, operating on you.

Imagine an elite athlete who never recovers. How long before muscles break down and stress fractures occur? It’s the same with your employees. If you want to reduce the risk of poorer performance, lower productivity, more accidents, or mistakes, make sure your staff is motivated, engaged, and well-rested. In such cases, shortening work hours while maintaining salary — and productivity — can create a sustainable workplace. It can also give you a competitive advantage in recruiting and retaining top talent. 

Right now, about 50% are considering changing jobs in the next year. Employers know this, but what do many do? They force staff back to the office five days a week... 

It’s not about laziness. It’s about working optimally. Recovery is super important. So yes, you can work shorter to get further — in certain professions. It’s worth thinking about and perhaps focusing on WHAT and HOW instead of IF.
