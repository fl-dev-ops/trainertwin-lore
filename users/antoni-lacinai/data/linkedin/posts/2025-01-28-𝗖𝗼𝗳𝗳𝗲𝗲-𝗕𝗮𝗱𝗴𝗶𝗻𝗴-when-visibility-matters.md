𝗖𝗼𝗳𝗳𝗲𝗲 𝗕𝗮𝗱𝗴𝗶𝗻𝗴 – When Visibility Matters More Than Productivity

A relatively new concept has emerged, where managers allow employees to work from home but clearly show dissatisfaction when employees are not in the office. It’s called 𝗰𝗼𝗳𝗳𝗲𝗲 𝗯𝗮𝗱𝗴𝗶𝗻𝗴, and in one study, 58% of employees said they had engaged in it at some point.

Imagine employees going to the office and swiping their cards to get inside. Once there, they make themselves visible, talk to people, drink coffee, and don’t really do anything productive before sneaking back out and heading home.

If it’s all about being seen in the office to gain favors (known in psychology as Proximity Bias), employees will show up—but only to make an appearance. They follow the unspoken rule: 𝗜𝘁’𝘀 𝗻𝗼𝘁 𝗮𝗯𝗼𝘂𝘁 𝘄𝗵𝗮𝘁 𝘆𝗼𝘂 𝗱𝗼 𝗯𝘂𝘁 𝘄𝗵𝗲𝗿𝗲 𝘆𝗼𝘂 𝗮𝗿𝗲. 

One way to address this is by forcing everyone back into the office five days a week. That’s the lazy solution. You haven’t tackled the motivation issue but are simply demonstrating low trust in your staff. 

I’ve seen many employees, long before the pandemic, who didn’t accomplish much other than dragging themselves to the office every day and pretending to work. So, that approach is often insufficient. It becomes a vicious cycle that’s hard to break. Punishing everyone who takes responsibility just because a few underperform is weak leadership.

Another way is to communicate a clear purpose, to empower employees to achieve their goals, to foster a sense of belonging, and to promote collaboration. You don’t lead with clenched fists but with an empathetic heart. You show that you care. You show trust and recognize achievements and individuals. Taking this path creates a better culture, more positive energy and creativity, more collaboration, and higher productivity. Having courageous conversations with the few who don’t pull their weight is good leadership.

But is workplace slacking really a new phenomenon? Not at all; it has existed throughout history. If you’ve worked in larger organizations, you may have wondered what certain individuals do all day. There’s no noticeable output, yet they’re visible, attend meetings, and talk to people. There’s an old anecdote about a worker in the Port of Gothenburg who walked around with a plank over his shoulder all day. He clocked in, grabbed his plank, chatted with people, clocked out, and went home. 

So, by all means, create opportunities for spontaneous meetings. Enjoy coffee. Chat about anything and everything. But also rewards collaboration, self-leadership, tasks completed, and shared goals.
