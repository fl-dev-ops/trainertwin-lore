𝗡𝗶𝗻𝗲 𝘁𝗶𝗽𝘀 𝗳𝗼𝗿 𝘆𝗼𝘂 𝘄𝗵𝗼 𝘄𝗮𝗻𝘁 𝘁𝗼 𝗺𝗼𝗱𝗲𝗿𝗮𝘁𝗲 𝗲𝘃𝗲𝗻𝘁𝘀

I used to moderate all the time, but nowadays I focus on my keynotes. Still, I want to share insights that will lift the event. It benefits all of us.  

𝟭. 𝗨𝗻𝗱𝗲𝗿𝘀𝘁𝗮𝗻𝗱 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗽𝘂𝗿𝗽𝗼𝘀𝗲 𝗼𝗳 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗲𝘃𝗲𝗻𝘁
Why does the event exist? What is it meant to achieve? What kind of atmosphere does the client want to create? How can you contribute to that?

𝟮. 𝗨𝗻𝗱𝗲𝗿𝘀𝘁𝗮𝗻𝗱 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝘀𝗽𝗲𝗮𝗸𝗲𝗿𝘀’ 𝘁𝗮𝗿𝗴𝗲𝘁 𝗮𝘂𝗱𝗶𝗲𝗻𝗰𝗲𝘀
Find out whether the speakers share the same audience or have different ones. You need to understand which audience each speaker wants to reach. Your role is to represent the audience while also helping the speaker shine.

𝟯. 𝗨𝗻𝗱𝗲𝗿𝘀𝘁𝗮𝗻𝗱 𝗲𝗮𝗰𝗵 𝘀𝗽𝗲𝗮𝗸𝗲𝗿’𝘀 𝗴𝗼𝗮𝗹 𝘄𝗶𝘁𝗵 𝘁𝗵𝗲𝗶𝗿 𝗽𝗿𝗲𝘀𝗲𝗻𝘁𝗮𝘁𝗶𝗼𝗻
What do they want the audience to know? What do they want the audience to feel? What do they want the audience to do?

𝟰. 𝗥𝗲𝘃𝗶𝗲𝘄 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝘀𝗽𝗲𝗮𝗸𝗲𝗿𝘀’ 𝗺𝗮𝘁𝗲𝗿𝗶𝗮𝗹 𝗶𝗻 𝗮𝗱𝘃𝗮𝗻𝗰𝗲
This allows you to identify interesting questions you can ask the speaker before or after their presentation.

𝟱. 𝗔𝘀𝗸 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝘀𝗽𝗲𝗮𝗸𝗲𝗿𝘀 𝗵𝗼𝘄 𝘁𝗵𝗲𝘆 𝘄𝗮𝗻𝘁 𝘁𝗼 𝗯𝗲 𝗶𝗻𝘁𝗿𝗼𝗱𝘂𝗰𝗲𝗱
Build their credibility. Set the right tone. If the client wants an informal atmosphere, you can, for example, include something personal in the introduction. Also, do your own research.

𝟲. 𝗔𝘀𝗸 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝘀𝗽𝗲𝗮𝗸𝗲𝗿𝘀 𝘄𝗵𝗶𝗰𝗵 𝗾𝘂𝗲𝘀𝘁𝗶𝗼𝗻𝘀 𝘁𝗵𝗲𝘆 𝘄𝗼𝘂𝗹𝗱 𝗹𝗶𝗸𝗲 𝘆𝗼𝘂 𝘁𝗼 𝗮𝘀𝗸
What does the speaker believe the audience wants to know more about? Dare to ask the “stupid questions.” The audience will thank you. When the audience is large, I advise skipping open Q&A sessions. Otherwise, there is a risk that a know-it-all will step in and ruin the atmosphere you and the speaker have built. On the other hand, if you have a positive atmosphere filled with trust; go for it!

𝟳. 𝗟𝗶𝗻𝗸 𝗼𝗻𝗲 𝗽𝗿𝗲𝘀𝗲𝗻𝘁𝗮𝘁𝗶𝗼𝗻 𝘁𝗼 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗻𝗲𝘅𝘁 – 𝗰𝗼𝗻𝘃𝗲𝘆 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗰𝗼𝗺𝗺𝗼𝗻 𝘁𝗵𝗿𝗲𝗮𝗱
The audience should feel completely safe with you as a moderator and sense that there is a clear reason for both the topics and the order in which they are presented.

𝟴. 𝗬𝗼𝘂 𝗮𝗿𝗲 𝗿𝘂𝗻𝗻𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗮 𝗺𝗮𝗿𝗮𝘁𝗵𝗼𝗻, 𝗻𝗼𝘁 𝗮 𝟭𝟬𝟬-𝗺𝗲𝘁𝗲𝗿 𝘀𝗽𝗿𝗶𝗻𝘁
The 100 meters are for the speakers. You will be on stage many times, so you need to manage your energy. Instead of giving 100%, 85% is enough — with full engagement, of course.

𝟵. 𝗘𝗻𝗱 𝗼𝗻 𝗮 𝗽𝗼𝘀𝗶𝘁𝗶𝘃𝗲 𝗻𝗼𝘁𝗲
Always end the parts you moderate in a major key, not a minor one. Do not let anyone finish with a negative comment before the speaker or the entire event concludes. That is what the audience will remember. Make sure they leave with a good aftertaste.

There you go. If you wanna dig deeper, I wrote a book about it, called Moderate great
