We wish each other a Happy New Year almost automatically.

Prosperous. Healthy. Joyful. Successful.
We offer these words warmly because it’s what we do at the turn of a year and perhaps because, beneath the ritual, we know what years are capable of.

A year, after all, is just a container of time.
Nothing more.

And yet a year can change us.

Looking back, it’s impossible not to notice how much has happened, some of it unwanted, most of it unplanned. Love and loss. Beginnings we didn’t anticipate. Endings we didn’t choose. Diagnoses. Opportunities. Disruptions. Very little of it arrived neatly or on cue.

Looking forward, the next year can feel oddly intimidating. We respond in familiar ways. Some of us meet it with goals and determination, as though clarity equals control. Others swing the opposite way, giving up on plans altogether, convinced by past delays that effort is futile and outcomes are fixed.

Many of us move between the two: planning hard, then meeting disruption, and quietly resigning ourselves when circumstances feel stronger than our ability to steer ahead.

These responses are understandable.
But they may be false choices.

A year will change us whether we plan for it or not. 

The question, then, is not simply whether we set goals or abandon them, but what sustains us when plans are disrupted and outcomes slip beyond our control.
Planning can tell us what we are aiming for. It cannot tell us how to live when life does not arrive on schedule.

This is where the distinction between maturity, humility, and wisdom begins to matter.

Maturity is about responsibility. It is the willingness to act where we can act, to take ownership of our choices, effort, conduct, and how we show up. It engages life with intention.

Humility is something else entirely. It is not passivity or self-diminishment, but the capacity to accept reality without defensiveness. To be taught by experience. To let go of narratives that no longer fit when life moves in directions we did not choose.

One can be responsible without being humble. One can be humble without being responsible. Wisdom asks us to hold the two together.

Wisdom knows where responsibility ends and where humility must begin. It is knowing how to live without ultimate control, while still taking responsibility for what is ours to carry.

Perhaps this is what a new year quietly asks of us. Not blind optimism. Not resignation. But a steadier posture: to act where we can, to remain open when we cannot, and to allow ourselves to be changed without becoming bitter or closed.

Because a year can change us.
It almost certainly will.

And if that is true, then perhaps the most generous wish we can offer each other is not a perfectly prosperous year, but an edifying and constructive one lived with the maturity to accept our responsibilities, the humility to remain open when our influence ends, and the wisdom to navigate what lies in between.
