Humour is often the last language barrier people notice

You’re in a meeting.
Or at lunch with colleagues.
Or on a call with an international team.

People laugh.

Not loudly.
Not politely.
Real laughter.

And you’re there, smiling…
nodding…
hoping no one asks you to explain the joke.

You didn’t miss the words.
You missed the 𝗺𝗲𝗮𝗻𝗶𝗻𝗴.

The timing.
The tone.
The cultural reference.

And in that moment, it’s not funny at all.

It feels like you’re on the outside of the conversation—
present, but not fully included.

𝗡𝗼𝘄 𝗹𝗲𝘁 𝗺𝗲 𝗳𝗹𝗶𝗽 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝘀𝗰𝗲𝗻𝗲.

Same room.
Same people.

This time, you make the joke.

Nothing fancy.
Just a light comment.
A small observation.

People pause.
Then laugh.

And suddenly:
- you’re not “the non-native speaker”
- you’re part of the group
- you’re connecting, not translating

That moment hits differently.

What matters more than people think:

To understand and use humour in English
you don’t need to be naturally funny.

You need:
- 𝗲𝘅𝗽𝗼𝘀𝘂𝗿𝗲 to real conversations
- 𝗽𝗿𝗮𝗰𝘁𝗶𝗰𝗲 with tone, timing, and context
- 𝗰𝗼𝗻𝘀𝗶𝘀𝘁𝗲𝗻𝗰𝘆, even when it feels awkward

You don’t get there by studying jokes.
You get there by being present, listening carefully, trying, missing a few… and trying again.

Just like confidence.
Just like fluency.
Just like leadership.

Humour is often the final signal that:
You’re no longer just speaking English.
You’re living it.

And yes, it takes work.

But the first time your joke lands…
You’ll know it was worth it.
