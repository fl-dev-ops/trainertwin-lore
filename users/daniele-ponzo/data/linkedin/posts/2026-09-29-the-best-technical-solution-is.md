The best technical solution is rarely the best business solution.

That took me years to understand.

When I worked in IT, I believed something many developers still believe today.

If I became technically stronger... my career would take care of itself.

So I studied.
I improved my technical skills.
I solved increasingly complex projects.
I even invested a huge amount of time improving my English.

I thought I was doing everything right.

Then something unexpected happened.

I watched colleagues with similar technical skills get promoted.

They were leading customer meetings.

Presenting solutions.

Working on strategic international projects.

And I kept asking myself:

What do they have that I don't?

It wasn't a better framework.
Or a deeper understanding of our solutions.

It was something much simpler.

They knew how to connect technology with business.

That changed the way I looked at communication.

This is something we should remember:

The best technical solution isn't always the best business solution.

An executive doesn't care that your architecture is beautifully designed if it delays the release by six weeks.

A client doesn't care that your code is elegant if they can't understand why it solves their problem.

A product manager isn't asking:
"Is this technically perfect?"

They're asking:
"Does this help us achieve the outcome we need?"

That's where many talented developers get stuck.

They struggle to explain trade-offs.

To translate complexity into clarity.

To help non-technical people make confident decisions.

And that's becoming even more important.

As AI makes writing code easier...

our value moves somewhere else.

Towards judgement.
Towards context.
Towards collaboration.
Towards communication.

I've seen this play out countless times.

Two developers propose similar solutions.

The first explains the technology.

The second explains the business impact.

Guess whose idea usually gets approved?

That's why I no longer think communication is a "soft skill."

It's an engineering skill.

Every important technical decision eventually needs someone to explain:

- why it matters
- what the trade-offs are
- and why it's the right decision.

The code gets built.

But communication is what gets people behind it.

I'm curious...

Looking back at your career, what has been harder?

Building the solution...
or helping other people understand why it was the right one?
