Structure is not a nice-to-have.
Sometimes it’s the difference between staying where you are… and moving forward.

A few weeks ago, one of my clients told me something very honest in our first session.

He said:

“When I speak in English, I lose the focus.
I start with one idea, then I add another, then another…
and I end up going in circles.”

Technically, he was strong.
Very strong.

But when he had to speak in English, especially in important situations, he felt ineffective.

Long sentences.
Too many details.
Trying to replicate his native language in English.

The result?

He knew what he wanted to say.
But the message didn’t land.

So we didn’t start with vocabulary.

We started with structure.

Simple, repeatable patterns he could rely on when the pressure went up.

Opinion → Reason → Example
Intro → Body → Conclusion
Point → Explanation → Impact

Structure became a grid.

Instead of inventing sentences,
he filled the grid with ideas..

Instead of translating,
he started thinking directly in English.

Instead of long, unstable sentences,
he used shorter, clearer ones.

And something important happened.

The cognitive load dropped.

Speaking felt lighter.
More controlled.
More predictable.

Last week he had a job interview.
A high-stakes one.

He told me afterwards:

“I didn’t get lost this time.
I knew where my sentence was going.
I used the structure.”

Result?

He passed the interview.
And secured a place in the final round.

Same person.
Same English level.

Different structure.
Different confidence.
Different outcome.

This is why structure matters.

Not to sound academic.
Not to sound perfect.

But to make sure that when the opportunity comes…
your message actually lands.

If you’re already working in English but your message doesn’t always land under pressure, structure might be the missing layer.

So don’t chase better English.
Build better structure.
