A software engineer working in English once told me:

“I need to improve my English.”

But after listening to him for 15 minutes, I realised the real problem was something else entirely.

His English wasn’t bad.

The problem was what happened under pressure.

In meetings:

he lost direction halfway through explanations
added too many technical details
kept expanding the sentence until the point disappeared
struggled to land ideas clearly and quickly

And over time, something frustrating started happening.

People interrupted him more often.
Management moved on quickly.
His ideas lost impact.

At one point he told me:

“I feel like my boss dismisses my contributions.”

That sentence had nothing to do with grammar.

It was about visibility.

Clarity.

Professional presence.

And the hardest part?

He was extremely competent technically.

He solved difficult problems.
Delivered results.
Understood everything perfectly.

But when it was time to explain those ideas in English under pressure…
the communication collapsed.

Because his brain was trying to organise, translate, structure, and perform…
all at the same time.

This is what most of the times stays hidden:

Technical skill loses value when communication collapses under pressure.

So instead of chasing “advanced English”…
we focused on making his communication easier to follow.

Shorter thoughts.
Clearer structure.
Less real-time overthinking.
Leading with the point first.
Repeatable speaking patterns instead of building every sentence live.

And slowly, things changed.

He stopped panicking mid-explanation.
Meetings became easier to manage.
People listened longer.

His ideas landed more clearly.
He sounded calmer.
More direct.
More senior.

That’s why I always say:

Most professionals don’t need perfect English.
They need better systems for speaking under pressure.

Because the goal is not sounding like a native speaker.
The goal is sounding calm, clear, and credible when the moment matters.
