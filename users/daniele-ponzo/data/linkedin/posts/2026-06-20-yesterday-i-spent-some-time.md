Yesterday I spent some time explaining bugs to children.

Not software bugs.
Actual bugs.

Although, for the software developers in my network, I realize this sounds like a very unusual debugging session.

I volunteered with a group of children from the local neighbourhood and showed them around our community garden.

We looked at the plants.
Talked about fruit and vegetables.
Explored the different corners of the garden.

And, most importantly, I introduced them to our bug hotel.

A small structure built to give insects a safe place to live, hide, rest, and quietly do the work that keeps the garden healthy.

The children were fascinated.

And, as children tend to do, they immediately started asking questions.

“What lives in there?”
“Why do they need it?”
“Are they good for the garden?”
“What happens if we don’t look after them?”

No jargon.
No overcomplication.
No trying to sound clever.

Just pure curiosity.

At one point I realised I was trying to explain an entire ecosystem using the simplest words I could find.

Which is surprisingly difficult.

Children are excellent at spotting when something doesn’t make sense.
If they lose track, you know immediately.

And honestly, I quite like that.

There’s something refreshing about conversations where nobody is trying to impress anyone.

People are just trying to understand.

That’s probably one of the reasons I enjoy gardening so much.

It slows things down.
You notice things.
You pay attention.
You realise how much life exists in places most people walk past without seeing.

And yesterday gave me exactly the kind of energy I enjoy most:

curiosity,
community,
fresh air,
good conversations,

and a reminder that simple is often better than complicated.

Also, I can now officially add:

"Explained bugs to children"

to my list of professional achievements.

Still waiting for LinkedIn to confirm whether this improves my credibility with software developers 🤷‍♂️ .
