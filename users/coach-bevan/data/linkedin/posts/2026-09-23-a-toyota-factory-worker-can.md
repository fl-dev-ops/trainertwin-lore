A Toyota factory worker can stop the entire production line.

They don’t need to wait for a senior manager. If they spot a fault, they stop it and fix it. 

Because running another 100 or 500 cars through the same fault creates a much bigger problem later.

Software development borrowed a lot from this thinking.
Agile came from manufacturing. Lean development came from the same place. But I think we’ve forgotten one of the most useful parts.

Sometimes you have to slow down to go faster.

There’s often such a rush towards delivery.
Get the feature into the sprint. Get it built. Get it released. Move on to the next thing.

Slowing down earlier can save months of work later. That might mean spending more time checking whether the feature solves a real customer need.

It might mean giving engineers space to think properly about the architecture.

Will this decision make future changes easier? Or will the team spend the next 2 years working around it?

The people who usually know when the line needs to stop are the people closest to the work.

The engineer who can see the technical problem coming. The Product Manager who has spoken to the customers.

The designer who knows the extra feature will make the product harder to use. 

But giving those people the power to say, “We need to pause here,” can make leaders uncomfortable.
A deadline might move.
A stakeholder might be disappointed.

The roadmap might need to change. The alternative is delivering something quickly that’s slightly broken, difficult to change and barely useful to the customer.

Toyota understood that fixing the line was part of moving quickly. Software teams could learn a lot from that.
