I’ve worked in several companies where Product Managers barely spoke to customers.

Senior management did most of the talking for them.

They’d worked in the industry for years, knew something about the customer and had strong opinions about what needed building.

Some of that knowledge was useful. Some of it came from when they were still working in operational roles years earlier. Their information was dated.

The Product Managers then made sure those ideas got delivered.
They adjusted requirements.
Moved tickets around.
Chased updates.
Sat with the development team. Made sure everything was implemented just right.

They looked busy. But they weren’t learning enough about the customer to represent them properly.

That’s a terrible use of a Product function.

The fix is uncomfortable. Pull Product out of delivery.

Fill their diaries with customer calls, visits and time watching people use their product.

Enough customer contact that they can form their own view. Delivery will miss them. 

Engineers may ask for them back. Deadlines can wobble. Hold. The. Line.

When Product Managers do every bit of admin for engineers, they can end up mollycoddling the team.

Once that support disappears, they start stepping up.
They arrange the meetings they need.
They move their own tickets.
They remove admin that never needed to exist.
The process gets leaner.

But there’s a painful period in the middle, before that happens. The old system creates a vacuum that keeps trying to drag Product back into delivery.
You have to get through it.

Eventually, engineers gain ownership.
And Product returns with the customer knowledge it was hired to bring. Nirvana.
