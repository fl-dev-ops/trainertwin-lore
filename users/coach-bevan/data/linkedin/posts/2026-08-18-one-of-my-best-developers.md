One of my best developers went quiet, and it was my fault.

He'd built something genuinely clever, and I wanted him to get the credit for it. So I gave him airtime with senior stakeholders, face time with the people who mattered, as a reward and a development opportunity. Good intentions all round.

What I hadn't thought through was what I was exposing him to. Up to that point I'd quietly shielded the team from the org politics above us. In that room, he saw it for the first time - stakeholders being careless, tearing chunks off each other, using his work as ammunition in fights that had nothing to do with the work itself.

It got in his head. I talked to him afterwards, validated him, tried to undo it. But something had shifted. He never really wanted to contribute in the same way again.

Here's what I took from it. Politics stops good people building things. Not because they can't handle it, but because watching your work become someone else's weapon makes the building feel pointless. And visibility, the thing we hand out as a reward, isn't always a gift. 
Sometimes the most valuable thing you do for your best people is stand between them and the noise.
