Removing a failing CEO is expensive. Not removing one is more expensive still.

Most of the financial modelling around executive failure assumes the CEO eventually departs. Severance, interim leadership, a replacement search, a second onboarding cycle — the numbers are significant, and I've written about them in detail. But that model is incomplete, because departure is not the only outcome of underperformance.

There is a second scenario that almost never makes it into the analysis: the CEO who stays.

Think about why most senior appointments are made in the first place. Organisations don't recruit a new CEO because things are going well and they want more of the same. They do it because something needs to change; a new market, a transformation, a culture shift, a business model under pressure. 

The new CEO was brought in specifically because the people already inside the organisation were too close to the problem, too shaped by the existing norms, to drive the disruption from within.

Now imagine that CEO is struggling, not dramatically, not in a way that forces a board decision, but visibly underperforming against that mandate. The growth targets are being missed by enough to matter but not enough to compel action. The transformation programme exists on paper but isn't landing. And because nobody wants to admit the appointment isn't working, the status quo continues.

What happens next is not neutral. It is actively, compoundingly negative. The behaviours and patterns the new CEO was brought in to disrupt, the functional siloes, the risk aversion, the cultural inertia, don't simply persist. They solidify. 

Every month without change provides fresh evidence that the old ways are the permanent ways. 

The transformation window, always at its widest immediately after a new appointment, gradually closes. By the time the decision is finally made, the organisation is harder to change than it was before the CEO arrived.

The exit costs of a failing CEO are significant. The entrenchment costs of a failing CEO who stays can be greater still.

Save yourself a lot of money; by spending a little bit more at the beginning.
