You can buy the AI. You can't buy the accountability.

This week the Monetary Authority of Singapore made that official.

On Wednesday, MAS issued its Guidelines on Artificial Intelligence Risk Management. They apply to every financial institution it regulates (banks, insurers, fund managers, payment institutions, exchanges, credit bureaus) and to every form of AI. Firms have until 7 October 2027.

Four things stood out for me:
1. The board and senior management own it. Clear roles, a risk appetite and a framework. You don't need a new AI committee, but someone has to be accountable.

2. You have to know where AI is being used. Every use, kept in an inventory, assessed for how material it is, with controls to match.

3. If you bought it from a vendor, it's still yours. MAS is explicit: firms remain accountable for AI "developed, operated or provided by third parties."

4. Controls scale with risk. Low-impact uses can run on basic policies. High-impact ones can't.

Here's what occurred to me as I read it.
Only recently a coaching client was telling me about people across their business going out and grabbing AI tools, then bolting them onto existing jobs. Ask an organisation like that to list every place AI is being used, and who is accountable for each one, and I suspect most would struggle.

The problem isn't actually one of compliance, but a deeper issue of organisational design.

To build that inventory properly, you have to map the work: the tasks, the decisions, who owns them, and where an agent is allowed to recommend, decide or act. That's exactly the work you'd need to do anyway to get real value from AI, rather than a few local productivity gains.

So the next twelve months can be spent in one of two ways. Producing a register to satisfy the regulator. Or using the same exercise to redesign how the organisation actually works.

The second costs much the same and is worth a great deal more.
