Organisations are consistently appointing the right people. Then systematically leaving them to fail.

The 70% CEO failure rate is not a selection problem. I want to be clear about that, because the instinct when you see a number like that is to conclude that organisations are choosing the wrong people. They are not. The search process is rigorous. The candidates are capable. The failure happens after the appointment, not before it — and it happens for a reason that is entirely structural and entirely predictable.

Every leadership career involves four distinct transitions: from individual contributor to leader of individuals; to leader of leaders; to functional head on a leadership team; and finally to CEO. Each one requires a fundamentally different set of behaviours. And at every stage, people get promoted based on what made them exceptional in the role they're leaving; not the one they're entering.

By the time someone makes the CEO transition, they inherit something that looks like a leadership team but often isn't. It is a group of functional heads, each managing their own domain, with very little operating as a genuine collective. For anything that crosses functions, and most things that matter strategically do, the CEO has to get personally involved. 

Meanwhile, 70–85% of those leadership team members don't have a ready successor beneath them. The new CEO isn't just navigating their own transition. They're holding together an entire system that is structurally underprepared for disruption at any level.

Every year, 15% of general management roles globally need to be filled. Yet 76% of companies don't have a ready-to-go successor when that moment comes. So the choice is always binary: promote someone who isn't ready, or hire from outside. Either way, someone is navigating the hardest transition of their career — and in 93% of organisations, they're doing it without formal support.

No wonder seven in ten don't make it through eighteen months.
