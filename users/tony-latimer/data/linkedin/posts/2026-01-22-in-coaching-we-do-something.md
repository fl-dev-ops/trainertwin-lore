In coaching, we do something very strange. We wait five or six years to start learning mastery, and then act surprised when the MCC evaluation exposes it.

And it explains why the MCC feels so brutal when people finally apply.

The journey to Master Certified Coach (MCC) status is a long and challenging one, and the reality is that most coaches fail the MCC evaluation at least once. The main stumbling block? Timing.

When coaches apply for their Associate Certified Coach (ACC) credential, almost everyone passes the first time. It’s a milestone people are happy to share. The Professional Certified Coach (PCC) application is similar—about 50 to 60% pass on their first attempt, and those who don’t usually just need to redo one component. But the MCC is a different story. The first-time failure rate is massive, and this has always been the case.

After many conversations with fellow mentor coaches and assessors, I realized a couple of years ago what’s really going wrong. The sheer number of coaching hours required to even consider applying for MCC is enormous. You need 500 hours to apply for your PCC, and accumulating the additional 2,000 hours for MCC typically takes five or six years, according to people I’ve spoken to at the ICF. During that time, you’ll renew your credential twice. By the time you’re ready to look at the MCC requirements, you’ve got the training hours and the coaching hours—the only missing piece is 10 hours of mentor coaching.

The reason for the huge jump in required coaching hours from PCC to MCC is simple: the leap in skill required is just as significant. These days, getting the additional training hours and CCE credits is easy. There’s plenty of material available, much of it free, which makes it even more attractive. But here’s the catch: almost all of it focuses on new, cool ways to apply your coaching skills at the PCC level.

The idea of signing up for a 75-hour level three course is daunting and expensive. They cost anywhere from $4,000 to $8,000.

We need an alternative. In other fields—take sports, for example—people are always training to reach the next level. No athlete would spend years practicing at the same level without pushing for improvement. What’s missing in coaching is the opportunity to start learning mastery as soon as you’ve earned your PCC. That way, by the time you’ve accumulated the required hours for MCC, you’ll have been coaching at a mastery level for quite some time. The evaluation process then becomes a confirmation of your skills, not a hurdle to overcome.
