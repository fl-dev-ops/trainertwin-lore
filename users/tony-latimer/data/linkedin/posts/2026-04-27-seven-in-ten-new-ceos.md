Seven in ten new CEOs fail to meet performance expectations within 18 months.

Not the obvious wrong hires. Not the ones who scraped through a flawed process. Seven in ten. The majority.

That figure comes from Gartner, Heidrick & Struggles, the Rock Centre at Stanford University, the Conference Board, and the Institute of Executive Development. 

It is not a fringe finding. It is what the best institutional research on senior leadership transitions consistently concludes.

Here's why it keeps happening. Only 7% of organisations formally support their C-suite with executive coaching. The rest do the same thing, every time: spend a fortune on the search, welcome the new CEO, hand them the keys, and hope for the best.

That hope, it turns out, is extraordinarily expensive.

I've spent over two decades coaching newly appointed CEOs through the transition that most organisations treat as though it doesn't exist. The failure rate doesn't surprise me anymore. What still surprises me is how little changes in response to it; in how appointments are structured, supported, and set up to succeed.

Over the next few weeks I'm going to be pulling apart what's actually behind that number: the full cost of getting it wrong, why the failure happens, and what materially changes the odds. If you work at board level, in HR leadership, or you've just made the move into a CEO role yourself, this is the conversation worth having.

I've written a full piece on what the data actually tells us: what it costs to make the appointment, what it costs when it fails, why the failure happens, and what changes the odds. If this number means anything to you — as a CHRO, a board member, or a CEO who's just made the move — it's worth the read.
