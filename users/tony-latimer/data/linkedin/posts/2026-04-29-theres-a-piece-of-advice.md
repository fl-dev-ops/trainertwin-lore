There's a piece of advice that gets passed between experienced executives and never gets written down. Here it is.

As you move up the corporate ladder, you get progressively better at negotiating your appointment package. Mentors share salary benchmarks. Predecessors pass on what they know about bonus structures and equity participation. By the time you're being headhunted for a CEO role, you arrive at the negotiating table well prepared on everything that affects day one.

What the most experienced people in the room know — and pass on quietly, selectively, to people they trust who are about to make a significant move — is this: spend at least twice as much time negotiating your severance terms as you spend on your joining package.

The logic isn't pessimistic. It's statistical. Your onboarding package is certain — it gets paid regardless of what happens. Your severance package is contingent; it may never be triggered. Except that the data says otherwise. Seven in ten new CEO appointments fail to meet performance expectations within eighteen months. The probability that your severance provisions will be invoked in your first two years is, actuarially speaking, substantially higher than the probability that they won't.

The specific terms — notice periods, the basis of termination, treatment of unvested equity, non-compete clauses, the precise conditions that trigger or exclude payment — are negotiable at appointment. They are vastly more difficult to contest at exit. Experienced practitioners in the executive search community understand this well. It tends to be less well understood by those making their first appointment at this level.

For the organisation, the same logic runs in the opposite direction. The severance package manages failure after it happens. 

Coaching prevents the failure from happening in the first place. 

Both are rational responses to the same underlying probability. One receives careful contractual attention. The other, in 93% of organisations, receives none at all.
