The Investment Decision Has a Half-Life

A perfectly sensible investment decision can become the wrong decision later. That's hardly controversial. What's stranger is how often the original approval keeps doing work long after the evidence that supported it has changed.

The investment went through IC. The thesis was debated. The cheque was written. Eighteen months later the company needs more money. The market is still attractive, but the company isn't winning as much of it as expected. The enterprise sales cycle is longer. A key customer dependency hasn't gone away. The founder hasn't built the management team the original case assumed would exist by now.

None of that proves the first decision was wrong. It does mean you have another decision to make. That's the distinction I think gets lost.

An original approval can gradually become inherited permission. The company is still backed because it's already backed. The thesis still stands because it was approved. The next cheque feels like a continuation of the first decision rather than a new use of capital.

I'd want to know what the original IC actually believed. Which assumptions mattered most? What were they worried about? What didn't they know? What would have made them look at the decision again? Six or eighteen months later, you should be able to compare those things with what actually happened.

Then ask a very simple question. If this business landed on our desk today, would we still make this investment? Maybe the answer is yes. Maybe you'd still invest, but not the same amount. Maybe you'd want something resolved first. Maybe the answer is no.

What matters is that today's permission comes from today's evidence rather than yesterday's approval. Conviction isn't supposed to change every time a number moves. You'd never invest in anything if it did. But neither should approval last forever simply because nobody decided when it should expire.

The first decision can have been right. You still have to make the next one.
