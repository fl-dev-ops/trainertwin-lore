Some B2B companies say they are customer-centric while paying as if the job ends at signature.

That is a strange place to stop rewarding judgment.

Because that is exactly where the customer’s exposure begins.

Not before the contract.  
After it.

In implementation.  
In adoption.  
In first use.  
In internal scrutiny.  
In renewal.  
In expansion.  
In the moment the promised outcome meets operating reality.

And yet many revenue systems still celebrate the earlier moment most heavily:

pipeline  
stage progression  
bookings  
closed revenue  
quarter attainment

Useful signals.  
But still early signals.

They show that something was sold.  
Not that it should have been.  
Not that it will work in the customer’s world.  
Not that the buyer will still look smart six months later.  
Not that the economics will still look good once support burden, concessions, delays, and rescue work appear.

So I keep coming back to this:

many B2B companies reward commercial commitment as if it were proof of customer value, when the real quality of revenue is only revealed later through implementation, adoption, retention, and expansion.

That creates a structural asymmetry.

The teams closest to the promise are often rewarded first.  
The teams closest to the consequences are left carrying what the metric did not see.

So the same deal gets described in completely different ways depending on where you sit.

Sales: win  
Implementation: risk  
CS: recovery  
Product: workaround pressure  
Finance: revenue  
Customer: still finding out whether the decision was wise

This is not an anti-sales argument.

The distortion is usually systemic, not personal.

Leadership wants the quarter.  
Finance wants predictability.  
Product wants time.  
Implementation wants realism.  
CS wants adoption.  
Partners want influence.  
Sales wants to get paid.  
The customer wants the thing to work.

All rational.  
Not well aligned.

This seems most acute in mid-market and enterprise B2B with higher ACVs, longer sales cycles, multi-stakeholder buying, implementation dependency, and growth models where renewal and expansion are supposed to validate the first sale.

So I want to pressure-test the thesis with people who have lived it.

Where is this wrong?

Which metrics are actively distorting behaviour today?

Pipeline?  
Stage progression?  
Win rate?  
Quota attainment?  
Forecast accuracy?  
NRR?  
GRR?  
Expansion?  
Partner-sourced revenue?  
CS health scores?  
Something else?

And what have you seen get rewarded that later became someone else’s problem?

The deal praised too early.  
The renewal won by concession.  
The expansion sold before the first use case worked.  
The partner deal delivery had to unravel.  
The forecast that was accurate to the booking date and wrong about realised value.

What is the earliest moment your company celebrates a deal that later turns out not to have been a good customer decision?
