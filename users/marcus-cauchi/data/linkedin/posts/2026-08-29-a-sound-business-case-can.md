A sound business case can still support the wrong decision.

You can show that a problem is real, put a credible financial value against fixing it and still be nowhere near knowing whether the organisation should commit. A business case tells you there may be value available. It doesn't tell you whether that value will actually appear, or whether the commitment being asked for is justified.

Start with what has to change. Is the result supposed to show up in revenue, margin, cash, capacity or reduced risk? Then work backwards. What has to happen for that result to occur, which of those conditions are already in place, and which are assumptions sitting inside the spreadsheet?

The organisation also has to be able to act. Having budget and someone with authority doesn't settle that. The change may compete with more important work, need people who aren't available, rely on behaviour changing or force people to confront something they'd rather leave alone.

Simon Rogers gave a good example recently. A data provider knew it had a serious quality problem, while customers were buying and relying on that data. Another company could help fix it and discussions got as far as quotes, but acting would also mean confronting what the poor data said about what had already been sold.

Simon said there were four prospects in that market and none of them would ever convert. The problem was real and there may have been genuine value in fixing it. That still didn't make them viable opportunities.

Then look at the supplier. Can they actually deliver what the result depends on? Does the product work under the customer's conditions, is the implementation capability there, and does success rely on clean data, scarce expertise, integrations or customer participation that may not turn up when they're needed?

A business case can look extremely convincing while assuming all of that away.

And even when the case for change survives those questions, you still haven't decided how much to commit. Evidence that some change is worthwhile isn't automatically evidence for the whole amount, scope or risk being proposed.
Sometimes the evidence supports the full decision. Sometimes it supports a smaller first commitment while an important assumption gets tested. 

Sometimes the sensible answer is to wait, change the terms or stop.

For a board, investment committee or commercial leadership team, the discussion has to get beyond, "Does the business case work?" They want to know what has to be true for the value to appear, what we actually know, what we're assuming, what evidence would cause us to change course, and how much commitment those answers justify today.

I've put that into a one-page Business Case Stress Test for boards and ICs. It's designed to be used against a live decision, not to generate another score.
