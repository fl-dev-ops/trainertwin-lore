How far ahead should the business be able to see?

Far enough to understand the risks and consequences of the decisions you’re making today.

You can have a twelve-month forecast and still make the wrong investment decision.
Suppose a new salesperson takes five months to become productive. Your forecast shows that you’ll need more revenue, but does it establish that hiring another salesperson will produce it?
Perhaps you don’t have enough people to serve the demand. Or perhaps you already have enough people, but too few customers are making progress towards buying.

Those are very different problems, and hiring more salespeople won’t necessarily solve either one.

The same applies to investing in AI, sales training, better data or changing how the team is managed. Each might help, but only if it addresses what’s getting in the way.

A healthy pipeline, a confident forecast and a busy sales team can create a reassuring picture. But how much of that confidence comes from what customers are actually doing?
Your salespeople may be making calls, holding meetings, sending proposals and updating the CRM. Meanwhile, customers may still be trying to agree what needs to change, whether it’s worth the money, who can approve it and whether they’re willing to act.

A forecast can tell you what you expect to happen. It doesn’t necessarily tell you why performance is disappointing or which intervention will improve it.

That matters when you’re deciding where to invest, what to stop and how much risk you can afford. When the business has debt to service, investors expecting returns and limited capital, even an intervention that improves sales activity or revenue may fail to generate enough cash to justify its cost.

And choosing the wrong intervention costs more than the money spent. You lose time, management attention and opportunities to do something more useful.

By the time the problem becomes obvious in the forecast or the bank balance, some of your better options may already have gone.

The real test is whether you understand what you’re assuming, what your customers are doing and what would make you change course while you still have choices.

How far ahead can your business see today, and which decisions are you making without enough evidence that they’ll produce the results you need?
