One idea from Dan Blake's post stayed with me.
 
Leadership can stabilise a weak system. It cannot replace one.
 
That distinction matters.
 
I've worked with enough organisations to know that capable leaders can compensate for almost anything, at least for a while. They ask better questions, challenge assumptions, calm situations and help people make better decisions under pressure.
 
The business gets through the moment. Everyone celebrates the leadership. Few people ask why leadership was needed in the first place.
 
That's where I think the conversation becomes interesting.
 
Every repeated rescue tells us something about the operating conditions. If the CEO keeps stepping into the same forecasts, the founder keeps saving the same strategic accounts or the executive team keeps resolving the same escalations, the organisation is signalling that capability still lives in people more than in the way the business operates.
 
That isn't a leadership problem. It's a design problem.
 
The strongest organisations I've worked with don't remove the need for judgement. They move judgement upstream. Leaders spend less time correcting individual decisions and more time designing the conditions that produce reliable decisions in the first place.
 
That's why I increasingly think governance is the consequence of operating conditions.
 
Not the meetings. Not the reports. Not the policies. Those reveal governance. They don't create it.
 
Governance begins much earlier. It begins with the conditions that determine what the organisation can be safely relied upon to do under pressure.
 
When confidence has to earn the right to be believed.
When evidence matters more than narrative.
When repeated rescue becomes a signal to redesign the system rather than another opportunity for heroics.
 
That's when leadership changes. Its role is no longer to compensate for weak operating conditions.
 
Its role is to make itself less necessary.
