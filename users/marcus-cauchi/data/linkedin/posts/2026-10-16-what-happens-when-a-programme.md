What happens when a programme is going to plan, but the original plan no longer makes as much sense?

Imagine you’re separating a business from its parent company. The programme is progressing well. The important systems are being separated, the necessary services are being put in place and the team is dealing with the problems you’d expect along the way.

Then you discover that something you expected to be straightforward will cost considerably more than anticipated.

Perhaps replacing a service previously provided by the parent will require more people or investment. Perhaps a capability you thought the new business could build quickly will take much longer. Or perhaps independence creates an opportunity that wasn’t available when the original plan was agreed.

None of those discoveries automatically means the programme is failing. They may be exactly the sort of things good programme management is supposed to uncover.

But they can change what makes commercial sense.

The original decision may have been perfectly reasonable given what was known at the time. The difficulty comes when people continue treating it as settled after the evidence supporting it has changed.

I’ve seen versions of this problem in commercial organisations. People are expected to deliver against commitments made under assumptions that no longer hold. They report the difficulties, explain the consequences and keep working towards the original target because that’s still what they’ve been asked to achieve.

Sometimes continuing is the right decision. Changing course can be more expensive, the evidence may be inconclusive, or the expected benefits may still justify the investment.

What matters is whether anyone has established that, rather than simply continuing because the decision has already been made.

There’s a difference between knowing that the plan has changed and establishing whether the original commitment is still justified.

For me, the useful question is what happens to new evidence once it reaches the people who can change the decision.

Can it genuinely change what the organisation does? What would justify continuing, changing course or stopping? And if the evidence isn’t strong enough to decide, what should be established before more money or time is committed?

A programme can be well managed and still produce information that challenges the decisions it was created to implement.

I’d want that information to improve the next decision, rather than simply become another problem for the programme team to manage.
