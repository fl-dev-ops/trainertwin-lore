Your Revenue Grew. Did Your Business Get Stronger?

Ask a leadership team what last year’s growth produced and they’ll probably start with revenue. Ask what that revenue cost to win and serve, or whether those customers are leaving the business better off, and the answer may take longer to put together

Most businesses hold some of that information. The trouble is that it doesn’t always influence the decisions and rewards that produced those customers in the first place

Think about what we’ve been conditioned to celebrate. The SDR who books the most meetings. The seller who brings in the most revenue. The marketing team that generates the most leads. The executive who delivers the biggest increase in turnover

Those achievements are easy to recognise. Their full consequences take longer to emerge, often in someone else’s numbers

A business can have excellent financial reporting and still reward behaviour that damages its economics. I’ve seen this repeatedly. In one company we worked with, the Board decided to increase the number of leads entering the sales pipeline to drive growth. Over 18 months, monthly marketing leads tripled from around 1,000 to 3,000

Over the same period, revenue fell to roughly 42% of its previous level. Final-stage conversion dropped from around 25% to 8%. Average discounting rose from 10% to 30%, and the sales cycle lengthened from 90 to 127 days. By the following year’s renewals, churn had risen from 11% to 23%

The figures only tell part of the story

Tripling the leads also tripled the number of people sellers were expected to follow up with. Making first contact could take anywhere from 5 to 23 attempts. That work had to come from somewhere

Sellers spent more time chasing leads and had less time for genuine opportunities. They were also under pressure to hit activity targets

In interviews, the sales team reported a substantial drop in morale. The CRM showed an average of just five contact attempts per lead. By the time we were invited in, 80% of leads had never had an effective contact

The Board had achieved what it set out to do. Top of funnel had tripled

But the sales team couldn’t reliably handle the volume. When revenue fell, the sales manager took the blame and the CEO began micromanaging the team. Accountability landed on the people dealing with the consequences, while the original decision to increase lead volume was never seriously revisited

The question that needed asking was whether more leads were the solution.
More leads meant more work. The results got worse

None of this makes growth the enemy. Businesses need to grow and invest. But getting bigger doesn’t necessarily mean getting stronger

More is not better. Better is better. More is just more

If we can’t establish whether yesterday’s growth made the business stronger, what are we using to decide how to grow tomorrow?

And when performance falls, are we examining the decisions that shaped it, or simply blaming the people expected to deliver?
