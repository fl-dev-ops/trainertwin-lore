Most founders don’t realise they’ve become the anchor.
It happens slowly.
And it starts as a good thing.

People looked to them because they were capable.
They knew the customers.
They could fix things in minutes.
They made good calls under pressure.
It felt right that everything flowed through them.

It felt safe.

But now the business is busier.
More moving parts.
More people.
More noise.
And somehow every road still leads straight back to them.

They wake up to messages.
Questions about things they didn’t ask to own.
Decisions that should’ve been made without them.
Little problems that grew because no one wanted to make the wrong call.

They tell themselves it’s fine.
“I’ll just sort it. It’s quicker.”
And it is quicker ... for today.

But each “quick fix” teaches the team one thing:
wait for the founder.

By midday they’ve handled six issues that aren’t theirs.
By the afternoon, they’re behind on the work only they can do.
And by the evening, they’re the last one still going.

They don’t complain.
They don’t blame.
But there’s a quiet thought they keep pushing down:

“Why am I still the one carrying all this?”

They’re not harsh.
They’re not controlling.
They’re tired.
They’re trying.
They’re doing what worked when the team was smaller.

But the truth is simple:
the anchor steadies the boat,
and also keeps it from moving.

The team waits.
Managers second-guess.
Decisions slow down.
And the founder becomes the bottleneck without ever meaning to.

They’re not the problem.
They’re the pattern.

And once they see it,
they can change it.
