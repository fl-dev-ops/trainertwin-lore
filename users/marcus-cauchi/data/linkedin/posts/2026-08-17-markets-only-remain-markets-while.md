Markets only remain markets while people can say no.
Democracy only remains democracy while governments can say no.
Rights only remain rights while people can enforce them.
Sovereignty only remains sovereignty while a country can choose another supplier, another investor, another technology and another course of action.

This applies just as much to governments, unions and public bodies as it does to corporations.
That sounds obvious. In practice, we rarely test it.

We argue about public versus private, left versus right, regulation versus growth, nationalisation versus markets. Those arguments matter, but they often begin one level too late.

Before deciding whether an arrangement is working, I’d ask six questions.

Can the customer genuinely leave?
- If there’s only one practical supplier, switching takes years, or the alternative means rebuilding everything around it, are we still dealing with a competitive market?

Can the government genuinely say no?
- If refusing an investor, platform or supplier threatens critical infrastructure, jobs, defence capability or public services, who has the stronger negotiating position?

Can a citizen enforce the right they supposedly possess?
- A right that requires money, specialist knowledge, years of litigation or personal risk to exercise isn’t equally available to everyone.

What have we become dependent on?
- Water, housing, cloud infrastructure, energy networks, healthcare capacity and payment systems can all generate legitimate returns. But if people cannot reasonably leave, what stops a fair return becoming a captive charge?

Who carries the downside when the arrangement fails?
 If one party keeps the upside while customers or taxpayers carry the failure, is the risk really private?

Could we reverse the decision?
- Not legally. Practically. Could another government change supplier, technology, ownership model or policy without years of disruption and enormous expense?

These aren’t arguments for more state or less state.
They’re tests of whether choice is real.

Markets need exit. Democracy needs power to be challengeable. Rights need recourse. Sovereignty needs more than permission to choose.
It needs the practical ability to choose differently.
