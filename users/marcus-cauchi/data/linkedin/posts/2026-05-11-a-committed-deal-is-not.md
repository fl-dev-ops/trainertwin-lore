A committed deal is not committed because the seller believes it.

It is only safer when the buyer has done decision work inside their own business. That means someone owns the problem, someone owns the cost of delay, someone understands the funding path, someone knows who has to approve it, and someone can defend the decision when challenged.

The wrong forecast question is, “Do we believe this deal will close?”

The better question is, “What buyer-owned evidence makes this safe to forecast?”

That question changes the review. It separates seller confidence from buyer movement. It protects the CRO from carrying hope into the number. It gives the CFO a cleaner way to test the forecast without turning the conversation into a fight.

The CFO test is simple. Which part of this forecast is supported by buyer-owned evidence, and which part is still seller confidence?

That is Commercial Reality in practice.

The issue is not whether the seller feels confident. The issue is whether the buyer has done anything that makes the confidence safer to trust.

Where does seller confidence most often get mistaken for forecast evidence?

Commit deals, best case, late-stage pipeline, renewals, or expansion?
