A founder is preparing to accept external investment. The numbers have been tested, the market has been challenged, and the leadership team has been examined. The founder has answered questions about growth, risk, resilience and ambition.

The accepted explanation is that this is normal diligence. The investor needs enough confidence to decide whether to back the company.

What may be missing is equivalent examination in the other direction. The decision is not only whether the investor should invest. It is whether the company should accept this investor.

The terms show what the investor is legally entitled to do. They may not show how the investor behaves when targets are missed, more capital is needed or the exit timetable changes.

That becomes expensive after the money arrives. Practical control may shift before ownership visibly changes, future funding choices may narrow, and board pressure may increase. The founder may still own the company while losing meaningful freedom over how it is run.

Not every investor creates this problem. A well-matched investor can add judgement, access, patience and resilience that the company could not build alone.

The useful test is:
What evidence do we have of how this investor behaves when the original plan stops being true?

Chemistry during the deal is useful. Behaviour under pressure is more useful.
Where the consequences are hard to reverse, the capital deserves at least as much diligence as the company receiving it.
