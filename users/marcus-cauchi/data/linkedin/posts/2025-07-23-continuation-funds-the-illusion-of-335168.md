Continuation Funds & the Illusion of Progress

You don’t need to sell a good company if it’s really that good. So why is private equity selling to itself?

Recent reports highlight a staggering £41 billion in continuation fund deals in the first half of 2025, revealing that these funds are no longer niche, they’ve become systemic. This phenomenon raises a critical question about the very nature of value in our economic landscape

This isn’t ownership; it’s narrative theatre. When I observe the machinations of private equity firms recycling their assets, I can’t help but dismiss this as mere performance optics masquerading as innovation. The practice of selling to oneself reflects a troubling trend, capitalism cannibalising its own credibility

Fees rise, wages stagnate. This isn’t merely a case of misalignment; it’s extraction. The burden shifts away from genuine productivity, leaving workers and customers serving the spreadsheet rather than the mission. We’re witnessing a systematic withdrawal of value creation, one that exacerbates income inequality and fragility across our economic framework

Printing financial illusions to comfort the already rich is a broader macroeconomic pattern revealing that the affluent are struggling to find real yield, leading them to recycle capital through increasingly absurd mechanisms that simulate returns. This behaviour epitomises a desperate attempt to maintain appearances in an environment devoid of genuine growth

This is a late-stage, trust-deficient system disguising decay. 

Is it just me who sees this as the erosion of trust and the dependency on synthetic liquidity as signs of systemic strain? 

Continuation funds serve as a tell that we are navigating the late stages of this cycle, where real economic activity is overshadowed by financial engineering

They’re marking up paper to distract from the emptiness of real growth. These funds are not building; they’re shuffling the deck while forcing the mark to take the card. This behaviour epitomises confidence without customers, where the focus is on maintaining appearances rather than fostering actual growth

It feels like success because it makes noise, but the bottle’s empty. It’s sound over substance. A glass ketchup bottle offers a satisfying "thunk" even when it’s empty. In the same vein, the illusion of success becomes more appealing than the reality of genuine value creation

As we dissect this complex web of financial performance, it’s essential to recognise the consequences for all involved. Top talent leaves. Customers notice. 

Value dies in silence.

If you’re building real value, let’s talk. If you’re flipping optics, move along. The next era belongs to those who earn #trust, not those who simulate exits.
