Elaine Barsoom is right about this. A policy, a committee and a board slide don't make AI responsible.
 
Several comments add another important point: somebody needs to own the decision, and they need enough authority to challenge or stop the system. I’d go one step further.
 
What happens after the AI has been approved?
 
Because approval happened at a particular moment, based on a particular model, use case, set of data, volume, level of access and expected consequence. Six months later, some of those things may have changed.
 
The model has been updated. More people are using it. It has access to another system. What began as a recommendation now triggers an action. The volume has increased tenfold. Somebody has found a useful new application that nobody considered when the original decision was made.
 
Nothing has necessarily “failed”.
 
But the decision we approved may no longer be the decision we are actually running.
 
That is where I think a lot of AI governance will get caught out. We are getting better at asking who is accountable when something goes wrong. We are much less good at asking whether the system still deserves the authority we gave it in the first place.
 
A named owner matters. So does a competent human who can understand what is happening, challenge it and intervene. But responsibility also means knowing what changes should force us to look again, reduce the system’s authority, or stop it altogether.
 
That is a different standard from saying the AI is “trusted”.
 
I don't particularly want people to trust consequential AI. I want them to know what they can reasonably rely on, where the limits are, and what evidence would cause us to change our minds.
 
The same applies at board level. Approving AI shouldn't mean approving it indefinitely.
 
What has this system actually earned permission to do today, and what would have to change for us to take some of that permission away?
 
If nobody can answer that, I'm not sure the organisation has governed the AI yet. It may simply have approved it.
