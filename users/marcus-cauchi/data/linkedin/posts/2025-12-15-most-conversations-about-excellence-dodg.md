Most conversations about excellence dodge the hard part.

They talk about mindset.
Or systems.
Or tools.
Even about the magic and wonder of AI.

They argue about which one matters more.

That argument is safe.
It lets people sound serious without paying for anything.

David Brock nailed the part most leaders don’t want to admit:

“Good enough” isn’t an accident. It’s a decision. You see it in the small stuff:

* Curiosity drops.
* Learning stops.
* Accountability gets fuzzy.
* Standards slide, and nobody names it.

You don’t drift into mediocrity. You cooperate with it. And tools don’t save you. AI doesn’t raise standards. It multiplies the ones you already tolerate. 

I often call this "scaling idiocy". If you accept shallow thinking, you get it faster.
If you avoid hard conversations, you automate the avoidance.

Where the advice usually lands is willpower:

“Choose excellence.”
“Raise the bar.”
“Lead by example.”

As if that stuff works in the real world. It's fine until real world pressure hits. Incentives bend. Fear shows up. Time evaporates. And good people start making compromises they swear are temporary.

That’s where systems matter. Not because people are weak. Because people are human. We get tired. We rationalise. We protect our status.

If you don’t design against that, “excellence” becomes a tax: the strongest carry it, and everyone else hides.

But here’s the part a lot of systems talk skips. Some people understand all of this and still choose “good enough.”

Not from confusion. From preference.

Because excellence costs them something they don’t want to lose:
- Comfort.
- Control.
- Cover.
- The ability to look competent without being tested.

No framework fixes that.

So the real question isn’t mindset vs systems.

It’s this: What are you willing to lose to stop tolerating “good enough”?

Because excellence takes something first, before it gives anything back.

If your system shields you from that cost, it’s broken. If your leadership language pretends the cost doesn’t exist, it’s dishonest.

If this landed harder than you expected, the question isn’t whether you’re failing. It’s where you’ve been paying for comfort without realising it.

The leaders worth following don’t beg people to be better. They remove the hiding places. Then they make the choice visible.

Not to shame. To clarify.

If “good enough” disappeared tomorrow, what would it cost you?
And would you still claim you want excellence if that cost had to be paid in public?
