Why do your people feel the need to game the system? Because leadership fails them. Period. Employees don’t wake up one morning and think, “You know what? I’m going to maliciously comply with some arbitrary policy for fun.” They do it because the environment you’ve created makes them feel ignored, powerless, and expendable. When you roll out tone-deaf mandates like sudden return-to-office policies with zero transparency or collaboration, you’re signaling loud and clear: “We don’t care about you. You’re just another cog in the machine”

And let’s talk about that machine. It’s built to squeeze every last ounce of productivity from employees while paying lip service to “culture” and “engagement.” You say you want collaboration, but what you really want is control. You don’t trust your people to deliver results unless you can watch them do it. That’s not leadership—it’s micromanagement masquerading as strategy

Gaming the system isn’t the disease. It’s the symptom of your leadership rot. When employees feel unheard, disrespected, or manipulated, they push back in the only ways they can. They’re not the problem—you are. You’ve created an environment where malicious compliance feels like the only way to protect themselves from arbitrary, out-of-touch policies that prioritize control over collaboration, optics over outcomes

So, how do you fix it? Here are three harmful practices to STOP immediately if you want to rebuild trust and engagement
	1.	Stop Making Abrupt, Top-Down Mandates Without Transparency
Abrupt decisions, like forcing everyone back to the office with no explanation, erode trust. Employees feel blindsided and undervalued
Instead: Communicate why decisions are being made, involve employees in shaping policies, and offer flexibility
Try pilot programs or phased rollouts that let people adapt while feeling included
	2.	Stop Ignoring Employee Feedback
If employees feel their concerns are falling on deaf ears, they’ll disengage—or worse, resist. Silence creates passive-aggression.
Instead: Actively ask for feedback and, more importantly, act on it. Use surveys, one-on-ones, or open forums to hear your team out. Communicate what you’re changing based on their input to show their voices matter.
	3.	Stop Prioritizing Control Over Outcomes
Micromanagement and rigid rules signal that leadership cares more about surveillance than results. This destroys trust and autonomy.
Instead: Focus on outcomes, not processes. Trust your employees to manage their workflows. Measure success by results—not how many hours they’re in the office.

People don’t game systems they believe in. They don’t resist leadership they trust. If your people are gaming the system, it’s because your leadership is a broken system in itself. Fix the system, and the behaviors will fix themselves. But until you’re willing to admit that, you’ll keep playing whack-a-mole with symptoms while ignoring the disease. The problem isn’t them—it’s you. Step up, stop the harm, and start leading.
