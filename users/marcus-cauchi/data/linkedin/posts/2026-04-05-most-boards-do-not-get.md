Most boards do not get blindsided because the signal was missing.

They get blindsided because the signal never became hard enough to ignore while there was still time to act.

That is the part people miss.

Not the miss.
The months before it.

The quarter still looks broadly fine.
The narrative still sounds intact.
The caveats still feel containable.
The room still leaves with more reassurance than discomfort.

And that is usually when the real danger gets protected.

Not by fraud.
By formatting.
By aggregation.
By confidence language.
By the habit of treating anything short of financial pain as “something to keep an eye on.”

A lot of weak revenue survives in exactly that gap.

Not because nobody saw it.
Because nobody forced it into one board-level truth early enough to change the decision.

The article below is about what a board should insist on seeing before the number starts lying by omission.

If you care about avoiding expensive surprises, late interventions, and the ritual sacrifice of another executive to a broken operating system, read on.
