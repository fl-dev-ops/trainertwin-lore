You can buy AI that works exactly as promised and still make a poor investment.

Adam Smith understood something we’re in danger of overlooking. A company’s commercial success doesn’t necessarily mean its customers are getting a good deal.

Look at how AI is being financed.
Some of the largest technology companies are simultaneously suppliers, investors and financiers. They provide computing capacity, invest in the businesses buying it and benefit from those businesses becoming more valuable.

There’s nothing inherently wrong with that. These arrangements may help build infrastructure that wouldn’t otherwise exist. But they can also create dependencies that become expensive to escape.

And that’s where this becomes your problem.
You’re considering an AI investment because you expect something to improve.
Perhaps your salespeople will become more productive. Your operating costs will fall. Your customers will receive better service.

What evidence tells you that the technology will produce those improvements?
What else needs to change for the benefits to materialise?
What will it cost to implement, operate and maintain, beyond the supplier’s price?
And what happens if the technology works, but the expected financial benefits don’t follow?

There’s another question worth asking before you sign.

What will you no longer be free to change?
Can you move your data? Replace the supplier? Reduce your commitment? Return to your previous arrangements without unacceptable disruption?

A contract may give you the right to leave while the way you’ve built your business around the technology makes leaving practically impossible.

None of this means you shouldn’t invest.

Waiting can be expensive too. Your existing systems may already be costing you more than replacing them would. But the decision shouldn’t rest on the supplier’s reputation, the excitement surrounding AI or a business case that only works when everything goes to plan.
Before you commit, establish what needs improving, whether the proposed investment addresses it and what evidence would justify changing course.

And if your existing team has already established those things reliably, don’t pay someone else to tell you what you know.

The question isn’t whether AI is worth investing in.
It’s whether this investment, on these terms, is worth making for your business.
