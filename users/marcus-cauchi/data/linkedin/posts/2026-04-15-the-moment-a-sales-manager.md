The moment a sales manager learns that accuracy creates friction and optimism buys time, the forecast stops being a tool. It becomes protection.

That is where the damage starts. Not in sales. In leadership.

Once the number gets softened on the way up, every decision built on top of it gets worse. Hiring moves too early. Capacity gets stretched on demand that is not real yet. Cash gets committed too soon. Delivery inherits promises it cannot keep. The board gets a version of the business that feels steadier than it is.

Then a quarter later everyone asks the same questions. Why is support buried? Why is churn rising? Why did revenue slip late again? Why are good people exhausted?

Because the truth was suppressed before anyone was willing to deal with it.

Sales managers are told to own the number, but not the reasons behind it. They didn't set the pricing. They didn't fix the product. They didn't create the delivery bottleneck. They can't make the customer ready. But they are still expected to keep the forecast intact.

So they do what the system rewards. They leave it in. Push the decision. Hope it lands. Buy another week.

Not because they are dishonest. Because the business has taught them that a number people can live with is safer than a number that's true. Once that takes hold, the forecast is not measuring performance anymore. It is managing internal discomfort.

That's why the damage always shows up later. The customer gets sold too early. Delivery inherits promises it cannot keep. Support gets flooded. Churn arrives downstream and gets treated like a surprise.

It wasn't a surprise. It was visible early. It just wasn't safe to say aloud.

That is what happens when a company rewards confidence more than accuracy.

Question for CEOs, CROs, and board members:

Where in your business have people learned that protecting the story matters more than telling the truth?
