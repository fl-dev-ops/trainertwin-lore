Partner leaders are often held responsible for failures that began long before the partner was recruited.

They are given a target, a list of territories and a growth number. Then they are expected to make the channel work inside a company that may not yet know how to work well with partners.

The offer is difficult to explain. The ideal customer changes depending on who is speaking. Direct sales can take a deal without consequence. Pricing is slow. Support depends on favours. Marketing material is written for the vendor rather than the partner. Product claims are made without enough thought about delivery.

The partner leader then spends the year translating, negotiating and repairing.
From the outside, that can look like relationship management. It is often system repair performed by one capable person who is trying to protect the partner, the customer and the forecast at the same time.

Good partner managers can keep this going for longer than anyone realises. Their judgement and personal credibility make a weak system look stronger than it is.

That creates a dangerous illusion. Leadership sees deals getting done and assumes the model works. The truth may be that one person is holding the whole thing together.

When that person leaves, burns out or stops compensating, the programme suddenly appears to collapse.

It did not collapse. The weakness was already there.

Before asking whether the partner leader is doing enough, ask whether the company has become one that a good partner can succeed with?
