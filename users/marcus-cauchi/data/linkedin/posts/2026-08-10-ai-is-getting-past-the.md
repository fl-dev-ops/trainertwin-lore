AI is getting past the “can we make this work?” stage. For many businesses, that question's already been answered. The tougher question is what the result now gives you permission to do.

A pilot can save time, improve output, increase revenue or reduce cost. That's useful evidence. It does not automatically mean you should scale it, change the cost base, alter the forecast or put the gain into the value creation plan.

Those decisions need a higher standard of evidence.

You need to know what produced the result, what would have happened anyway and how much of the gain still depends on unusual levels of attention or intervention. You also need to know what has to remain true once the experiment becomes part of normal operations.

This gets harder when successful experiments become repeatable playbooks. Repeatability is valuable, but it also means a weak assumption can travel across several businesses before anyone discovers how much of the reported value depended on it.

There's a distinction here that matters.

Value identified, value realised and value you are prepared to rely on are not the same thing.

The difference only really becomes visible when someone wants to put another decision on top of the result. That might mean more investment, lower headcount, a stronger forecast, a larger value creation claim or greater operational dependence on the technology.

At that point, “does it work?” is no longer the useful test.

The question is what the available evidence is strong enough to support, and what would have to change before that decision should be reopened.
That is the line I keep coming back to.

When does value identified become value you are prepared to rely on?
