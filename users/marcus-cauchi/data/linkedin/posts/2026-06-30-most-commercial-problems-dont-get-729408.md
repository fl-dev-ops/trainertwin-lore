Most commercial problems don’t get solved. They migrate. The question is, what is the most expensive work in your business that should never have existed, and where did it really begin?
  
Sales calls it pipeline quality. Customer Success calls it post-sale repair. Finance calls it forecast risk. Managers call it deal rescue. The board calls it missed execution. Different names, same underlying condition.
  
Each problem is usually treated as separate. Separate meetings. Separate projects. Separate owners. Separate budgets. The work changes, but the pattern survives. Sometimes it returns in the same place. More often it reappears somewhere else in the commercial operation.
  
Waste rarely stays where it starts. One function creates it. Another amplifies it. A third absorbs it. Eventually somebody pays for it. Weak qualification becomes proposal rework, forecast volatility, discounting, onboarding friction, margin erosion, customer churn, management firefighting or another difficult board meeting. Everyone experiences the symptoms. Few can see the chain that connects them.
  
Pressure creates targets. Targets create measures. Measures make certain behaviours rational. Those behaviours become accepted ways of working. Before long, they stop being questioned. The waste becomes normal, and improvement efforts focus on managing symptoms instead of removing the conditions producing them.
  
That is why so many transformation programmes disappoint. They improve one part of the system while increasing the burden somewhere else, without anyone seeing the full chain. One team’s success metric becomes another team’s workload. Capacity disappears into work that should never have existed, and because the cost is spread across functions, nobody sees the whole picture.
  
I’ve been turning this into a commercial diagnostic that follows the waste, not the org chart. It traces where preventable burden is generated, who amplifies it, who absorbs it, who pays for it, and where the earliest intervention would remove the greatest amount of unnecessary work.
  
The organisations I’m looking for already know something is wrong. They have invested in improving commercial performance, yet the same frustrations keep returning under different names. They value evidence over opinion, have access to operating data, and are prepared to challenge assumptions that have become accepted simply because they have been there for years.
  
I’m only taking a small number of these because the work has to be done properly. If you have live evidence and the problem matters enough to examine seriously, message me with WASTE and tell me which commercial problem refuses to stay solved.
