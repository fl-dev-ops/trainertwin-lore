The investor is diligencing your company.

Who is diligencing the capital?

A term sheet makes the offer visible. You can see the valuation, the amount of money and the investor’s name.

It does not show you the company you will be running after you accept it.

Your lawyer can explain the clauses. Your accountant can explain the figures. Your corporate finance adviser can explain the process. Each may be doing excellent work, and you can still be left to join control, authority, debt, preferences, disclosure, rollover and the bad case for yourself.

That's where the expensive surprises tend to begin.

The founder keeps the CEO title but loses control of the decisions needed to deliver the target. Fifteen per cent survives on the cap table and produces very little in the waterfall. Debt remains serviceable while the freedom to recover has already moved elsewhere.

The good case sells the deal.
The bad case shows you the deal.

I have written the opening article in a seven-part series for founders approaching institutional capital.

It is not a fundraising guide and it is not an argument against investors. It examines the decision sitting underneath the offer before signing, exclusivity, disclosure, debt, rollover or a new set of controls makes it difficult to reverse.
The article sets out seven questions I would want answered before taking the money and introduces a short self-audit built around the founder’s own documents and operating reality.

Read Before You Take the Money.

Then look at your transaction and ask which important part still depends on something said, assumed or left for later.
