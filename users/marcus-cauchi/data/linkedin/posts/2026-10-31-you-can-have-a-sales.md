You can have a sales team doing exactly what you’ve asked them to do and still be disappointed with the results.

They’re making the calls, sending the emails, booking meetings and following the process. They’re doing what you’ve trained them to do, what you measure and what you reward.

So when the results aren’t good enough, who’s responsible, and what should you change? You might decide to hire more salespeople, replace the manager, invest in training or introduce AI to improve productivity. Any of those decisions might be right, but they address different problems.

Suzanne raises an important point in this article. We keep criticising salespeople for poor outreach when they’re often doing precisely what management expects of them.

There's real consequence here that deserves more attention, particularly if you’re the one approving the budget. Your activity reports can tell you whether people are following the process. They can’t, on their own, establish whether that process is helping you win and keep the right customers. 

And if you haven’t established that, how do you know what needs fixing? You could end up replacing perfectly capable people, changing something that was working or spending more money making an ineffective process faster.

None of that means the original decisions were necessarily wrong. But when the results aren’t what you expected, carrying on without questioning the assumptions behind them can get expensive.

That’s why Suzanne’s article is worth reading.

It’s not just about why salespeople keep sending messages nobody wants. It’s about how the decisions we make as leaders shape the behaviour we get, and whether we’re paying for activity or something that genuinely helps the business and its customers.
