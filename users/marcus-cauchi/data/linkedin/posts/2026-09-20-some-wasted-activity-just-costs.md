Some wasted activity just costs you money. Some of it actually makes things worse, and that’s the cost nobody seems to count.

A lot of sales targets start with what the financial model needs and work backwards from there. Once that number gets handed to sales, activity is one of the easiest things to inspect. Listening properly to calls takes time and judgement. Counting dials takes seconds. So we take some of the least experienced people in the business, give them a list of strangers and tell them to make 80 or 100 calls a day. Before long, the measure that was supposed to predict the outcome becomes the outcome.

And some of those calls aren’t just wasted. A badly aimed call gives another buyer a reason not to answer the next one. It teaches them to ignore us before we’ve got anything useful to say. It teaches the rep as well. Make the dial, get through the list, get the meeting, move on. Do that for long enough and don’t be surprised when they finally get in front of someone who might buy and they can’t work out what’s really going on.

Then the results come back poor and we raise the activity target. More calls. More emails. More pressure. More of the thing that already isn’t working, without ever checking whether the problem was too little activity in the first place.

There’s a cheap way to find out. Before anyone touches the target, pick twenty calls and actually listen to them. Who are we calling and why them? What did the rep know before they picked up the phone? Was there a decent reason for that person to care? What happened when they answered, and what did we learn?

Then decide whether the problem really is that they aren’t making enough calls.
