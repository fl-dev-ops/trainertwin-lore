Companies may be saving money on entry-level work while destroying the route by which experienced judgement gets made.

That bill will arrive later, when the people expected to challenge AI have never had to do the work badly, spot what went wrong or live with the consequence.
Jeff Raikes is right to raise the risk to the talent pipeline. The problem is bigger than fewer junior jobs. Many of those roles are where people learn how the business actually works, where the data lies, what customers leave unsaid, which shortcuts create trouble and when an apparently sensible answer should be challenged.

Leaders are being told that human judgement will become more valuable as AI takes on more work. At the same time, they are automating the work through which that judgement used to develop.

That creates a nasty contradiction. You can remove the cost of apprenticeship now, then discover in five years that nobody knows enough to supervise the machine, challenge the brief or take responsibility when the answer is wrong.

The question is not simply which junior tasks AI can perform.

It is which work people still need to do, struggle with and learn from before the organisation can safely depend on their judgement later.
Otherwise, we are not replacing work.

We are borrowing productivity from the next generation of capability.

That is strong enough to justify the reshare because it gives your audience a decision they now need to examine:
Which work are we automating, and what future capability was that work quietly producing?
