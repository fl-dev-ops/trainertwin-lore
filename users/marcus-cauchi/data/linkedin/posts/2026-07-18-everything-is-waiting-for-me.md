"Everything is waiting for me"

He said it as though he was admitting something shameful.

“Nothing moves unless I get involved.”

There was no pride in it. He looked tired. His phone kept lighting up while we spoke and every time it did, his eyes dropped towards it before he caught himself.

A customer wanted a concession. A manager needed approval. Someone had written an email and wanted him to “cast an eye over it”. A difficult deal had stopped moving because the team wanted him on the next call.

None of the requests looked unreasonable. That was the problem.

Dependence rarely arrives as one dramatic failure. It builds through hundreds of sensible interventions.

You step in because the customer matters. You rewrite the email because it will only take five minutes. You make the decision because the manager is struggling and there is too much at stake to let them get it wrong.

Each rescue saves time today.
Then one morning you realise the organisation has learned to wait for you.

The team calls it support. Customers call it access. You call it being responsible.
Your family calls it being late again.

The better you are at rescuing things, the harder it becomes to stop. Your judgement is quicker. Your answer feels safer. You know the history, the customer and the political sensitivities. Letting someone else work through it feels slower and riskier.

So people ask. You answer. The business moves.

Until you are exhausted, ill, away or simply no longer willing to live like that.
Leaders often describe this as a lack of ownership. Sometimes it is. Sometimes the team has never been given clear authority. Sometimes people have made decisions before and had them reversed, criticised or pulled apart in public. Sometimes asking has become safer than thinking.

Telling them to “step up” won't fix that. You have to inspect what happens when they try.

I asked him to show me the decisions and approvals that had reached him during the previous two weeks.

There were twenty-three.

Four genuinely required his authority.
He stared at the other nineteen for a long time.
