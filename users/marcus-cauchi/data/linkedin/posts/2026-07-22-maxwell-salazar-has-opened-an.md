Maxwell Salazar has opened an important discussion about what happens to CEOs when a five-year private equity hold becomes seven, eight or ten.

The comments have added a useful distinction. Before changing the CEO, the board must separate leadership failure from thesis failure, operating-system failure and execution failure. That diagnosis matters, but it still leaves a harder question unanswered.

What happens after the board discovers that the original investment thesis no longer holds?

A seven-year hold is rarely the original five-year decision with another two years added. The cost of capital may have changed. The expected buyer may no longer exist. The growth assumptions may have failed. The management incentive may have lost credibility. The capabilities needed for the next phase may be different from those approved at entry.

The CEO may still be judged as though the mandate itself has not changed.

That is where a leadership problem becomes a governance failure.
Boards can acknowledge changed conditions, revise the budget, refresh the value-creation plan and demand a new forecast. None of those actions necessarily answers whether the original mandate remains valid.
Review is not reauthorisation.

Someone must preserve the basis on which the investment and leadership mandate were approved. Someone must identify which material assumptions still hold, which have failed and which remain unproven. Someone must have explicit authority to decide whether the mandate continues, changes or stops.
Without that discipline, the thesis survives by default.

The next CEO then inherits responsibility for producing an outcome that may no longer be obtainable. The sponsor changes the person while preserving the decision. The new leader gets less time, more pressure and the same unresolved constraints.

This problem may become more acute as private equity separates.

Megafunds can buy operating capability, create more liquidity options, move talent across portfolios and absorb longer holds. Genuine specialists can earn confidence through focus, access and repeatable expertise.

The exposed middle has fewer protections. Many firms face ageing assets, weaker distributions, harder fundraising and greater operating demands. They cannot outspend the largest funds, and without clear specialisation they may struggle to explain why scarce LP capital should choose them.

That does not make failure inevitable. It does increase the pressure to keep relying on assumptions that can no longer be evidenced.
