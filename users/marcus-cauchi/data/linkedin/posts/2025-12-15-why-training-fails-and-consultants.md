Why training fails and consultants fall short


I don’t think training is pointless. I believe it’s been asked to do a job it can’t do.

Most teams already know roughly what “good” looks like. They’ve sat through the sessions. They can repeat the frameworks. They’ve got the certificates somewhere in a drawer.


Then the quarter gets tight. Pressure and fear creep in. And people do what the system rewards. That’s where training breaks.


Training happens away from the work.

Selling happens in the middle of it.

With real risk.

Real consequences.

And very little patience for theory.


What you learn in a room doesn’t survive a live deal. Role plays don’t recreate fear. Frameworks don’t override incentives. Certifications don’t cancel quota pressure or a panicked boss. So behaviour snaps back. Not because people don’t care. Because structure always beats intention.

Consultants fall short for a similar reason. They’re brought in to explain what’s going wrong. They’re rarely around long enough to live with the consequences. They diagnose. They design. They present ... then they leave.

But sales systems don’t change on slides. They change through daily correction. Through holding the line when it’s uncomfortable. Through saying no when the numbers are shouting yes.

Consulting works when the problem is technical. Sales failure rarely is. Judgement is local. It’s built in context. It grows slowly. And it decays if it isn’t exercised.

No external team can resolve a conflict of interest for you. No playbook can compensate for broken ownership. No transformation programme can make incentives behave. Most organisations end up retraining the same problems every year.

Same workshops. Same decks. Same issues. Different logos. That’s not learning. That’s theatre.

Both training and consulting are built on the hope that if you explain things clearly enough, behaviour will change. In reality, people do what makes it easiest to survive inside the system they’re in.

The organisations that actually fix this don’t train more. They don’t explain harder. They change how the work itself is structured. Change sticks when ownership is clear. When incentives line up with the shared, ideal outcome. When learning happens inside real work, not around it. When feedback is immediate and lived ... and when poor behaviour actually has a cost.

That isn’t a training job. And it isn’t an advisory job. It’s structural work.

Fix the structure and people learn without being taught.
