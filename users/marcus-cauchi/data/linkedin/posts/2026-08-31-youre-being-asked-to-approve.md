You’re being asked to approve the next step. The next tranche, the renewal, the follow-on round.

Maybe the numbers stack up. Management has a decent explanation. The forecast has genuinely improved. The plan is starting to work.
What would have to be true for you to say yes? And which of those things do you actually know?

Some of it will be backed by decent evidence. Some will be reasonable assumptions. Some will only look solid because it has been repeated often enough to feel that way.

Now take the important ones. If one turns out to be wrong six months from now, would you still approve the same amount, on the same terms, today?

Maybe you would. If so, that matters. It tells you the decision may be stronger than it first looked.

But the evidence might support the next step without supporting the whole commitment. It might support the money, but only against conditions. It might support another quarter, but with a different test. One missing piece of evidence might be enough to change the decision.

That is where the judgement sits. Is this a good idea? Do the numbers work? Does management believe it? All useful questions. But they still leave one unanswered.

How much of this decision does the evidence actually support?

The answer might be all of it. It might be less. It might be the same decision on different terms, or a reason to resolve one thing before going further. 
Sometimes the most useful result is finding that the original decision still stands.

Know how far the evidence lets you go before more money, time, capacity or future choice gets locked in.
