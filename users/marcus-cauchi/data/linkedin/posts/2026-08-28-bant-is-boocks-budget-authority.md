BANT is Bo££ocks.

Budget, authority, need and timing are useful things to know. Of course they are. But plenty of SDRs are still taught to BANT-qualify people as though getting those four answers means the opportunity is worth progressing.
It doesn’t.

Someone can have the money, the authority, a real problem and a desire to act now, and we can still be the wrong supplier. They can tick every box and still be on their way to making a bad decision.

A lot of discovery makes the same mistake. Find the pain. Work out what it is costing. Find the budget. Find the decision-maker. Once those things are in place, the deal starts looking better from our side.

But pain doesn’t mean someone should buy. It means something may need looking at. The thing hurting may be a symptom rather than the real problem. It may not be worth fixing now. Fixing it may cause more cost and disruption than leaving it alone.

Having budget doesn’t mean spending it is a good idea. Having authority means somebody can sign, not that the people who have to live with the decision are ready for it or support it.

Even “need” is much weaker than it sounds. A customer can have a problem we genuinely solve and still have a better option. It could be another supplier. It could be something they can fix themselves. It could be waiting. Sometimes the right answer is not to buy anything yet.

And timing? Wanting this sorted this quarter doesn’t mean this quarter is the right time to do it.

That is what bothers me about seller-first qualification. We start trying to work out whether there is a deal for us before we have really understood the decision the customer is trying to make.

The customer is trying to work out something else entirely. What are we actually trying to change? Is it worth changing? What happens if we do nothing? What are the other ways of solving it? Can we make this work here? What happens to us if we get it wrong?

Then we can start asking whether we belong in the answer.

Are we actually the right supplier? Can they get the result they are paying for with us? Is there a better answer? Are they ready to make it work?

And before any of that, there is one question we do not ask often enough.

Should they buy anything at all?

That is the difference for me. Seller-first qualification asks whether they can buy from us. Good selling starts by asking whether they should do anything, then whether we are the right people to help.

A good seller has to be able to say, “They could buy from us. I don’t think they should.”

And if someone on your team says that, the first response should not be, “Your job is to progress the opportunity.” It should be, “What have you seen?”

Pain is not a deal. Budget is not value. Authority is not readiness. Need does not mean we are the right answer. Timing does not mean now is the right time.
BANT tells you whether a sale might be possible.

It tells you very little about whether the customer should make it.
