Who is left to pay the negative price for your positive pay-off

Instead of asking “Can we close this?”
what would change if we asked
“Would I still sell this if I only got paid after it worked?”

Not after signature.
Not after go-live.
After a couple of years, when the customer comes back and says, yes, this actually delivered what we thought we were buying.

Run that question across enough deals and the pattern stops being subtle.

Most pipelines don’t survive it.

Not because people are lying.
Because it’s easy to sound confident when you know you won’t be around when the consequences turn up.

When sellers get paid to sell and run, bad behaviour starts to feel necessary to survive, so don’t be surprised when salespeople optimise for speed of decision, not the right decision.

Anything awkward gets parked as “we’ll sort that later” or “not our problem”.

When your commission cheque clears before the damage shows up, it’s amazing how reasonable pushing complexity downstream, skipping hard conversations, and calling hope a plan start to feel.

The deal looks fine.
The real work shows up afterwards.

The rework.
The workarounds.
The manual fixes.
The quiet compensations nobody budgeted for.

And by then, you’re long gone.
Cheque banked.

Inside the customer.
Inside teams that never asked for the tool.
Inside workflows now bent to make an old decision look less wrong.

Across thousands of real-world repeats, one thing dominates everything else.

Whether the seller still has skin in the game after the deal is done.

If you had to wait to get paid, you’d slow down.
You’d ask the questions you currently avoid.
You’d walk away from deals that look good but won’t survive contact with reality.

Not because you’ve become noble.
Because you wouldn’t want to be the one stuck living with the outcome.

Most bad decisions don’t feel reckless at the time.
They feel sensible because someone else will be left holding them.

If your model only works because you get paid before reality shows up, the problem isn’t execution.
It’s where the risk ends up.

So ask the question in your next deal review.

Who is left to pay the negative price for your positive pay-off?

What collapses when you ask it is usually the story holding the deal together.

Some people will call this anti-sales.
Some will say it’s unrealistic.
Others will insist it would never scale.

That’s not a criticism. It’s an admission.

It’s an admission that the current model only works if someone else carries the mess.
It’s an admission that speed matters more than sense.
It’s an admission that scale depends on getting paid before reality turns up.

If a way of selling collapses the moment you’re asked to live with the outcome, the problem isn’t the idea.
It’s the system you’re defending.
