Most people don’t hate buying. They hate suspecting the decision serves the seller more than them.

We praise salespeople by saying they could sell water to a drowning man. It’s meant as a compliment, but it’s really a confession about getting people to agree to what they don’t need.

Sixteen years ago, I worked with Jenny Plant  at Publicis. She was being prepared for greater responsibility while her father fell ill, whilst she was  accountable for results she couldn’t control.

Jenny says our first conversation made her cry, not because I frightened her, but because I asked questions beneath the polished performance, into what she was being asked to inherit. She thought, “Who the hell is this bloke?” Then realised I genuinely cared what the role might do to her unprepared.

Soon after, Jenny learned a major client was about to put a six-figure account out to pitch. Her instinct was to panic and save it. Instead, we looked at it from the customer’s side: they faced a launch, and replacing the agency meant months of relearning at the worst moment. Jenny raised this, and they postponed the pitch for months.

Then they left anyway.

Most sales stories would hide that ending. The loss didn’t make the conversation a failure. Jenny had protected the client from disruption at the wrong moment. The transaction ended, but the value of the judgement remained.

Jenny fell in love with selling, although what really happened was that she discovered what selling could be.

She loves people, is naturally curious, and prefers listening to talking. Good selling gave those instincts somewhere to go: better questions helped her see what was happening in someone else’s world.

She didn’t fall in love with persuasion. She fell in love with the moment someone feels safe enough to tell the truth and make a better decision.

That discovery became part of a larger decision. Jenny wanted more control over her life, more time with her father, and the freedom to do work that mattered, her own way. She left and built the business she’s run since 2010.

“It was life-changing,” she told me.

A good seller is a mirror. It doesn’t flatter or decide for you, it shows what’s there and lets you choose with your eyes open.
