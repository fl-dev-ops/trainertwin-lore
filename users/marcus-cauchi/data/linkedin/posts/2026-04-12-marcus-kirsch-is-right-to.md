Marcus Kirsch is right to challenge the idea that sales creates revenue. But I think the deeper issue is that most companies still manage growth as a set of separate functions instead of one joined-up system.

Marketing shapes the promise. Sales secures the commitment. Delivery proves whether the promise was worth making. Leadership decides whether those parts work together or just hand problems to one another.

That's where the leak usually starts.

A business can look commercially strong because demand is coming in and deals are getting signed. But if the expectation set in the market is too broad, the sale is too loosely qualified, the handover is vague, or nobody owns adoption after the contract is signed, then the revenue was never as solid as it looked.

It was conditional from the start.

That's why this is not really a sales problem on its own, and not just a post-sale problem either. It is a continuity problem.

The promise gets made in one part of the business.
The customer tests it in another.
The commercial consequences show up somewhere else.
And leadership often discovers the gap too late, when churn rises, renewals get harder, expansion stays thin, and sales is pushed to replace revenue the business should have kept.

That's when growth starts lying.

Booked revenue looks healthy. But underneath it, accounts stay shallow, customers never fully embed, and the return on acquisition is weaker than the headline suggests.

More sales can hide that for a while. It can't fix it.

In fact, if the system behind the sale is weak, pushing harder on acquisition usually makes the economics worse. You're not building on strength. You're feeding more volume into a model that still struggles to turn first commitment into lasting value.

The better companies understand this earlier.

They don't just ask whether they are winning customers. They ask whether the whole path from market promise to lived customer experience is tight enough for revenue to hold, renew, and grow.

That shifts the question.

Not: How do we sell more?

But: Where does our business break the link between promise, delivery, and retained revenue?

That's usually where the real growth problem is.

Question for CEOs, operating partners, and board members:

Where in your business does a signed customer most often fail to become a working relationship that lasts?
