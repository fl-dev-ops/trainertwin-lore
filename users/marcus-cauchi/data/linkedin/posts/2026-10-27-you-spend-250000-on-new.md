You spend £250,000 on new technology. The business case looks good. The numbers add up. Everyone agrees it makes sense. It’s going to save 400 hours a week. Wonderful.

Six months later, where did those 400 hours go? Did you save any money? Get more work done? Serve customers better? Avoid taking on more people? Or did everyone just get busier doing something else?

Will Linssenen made a good point in the comments on my original post. There’s a world of difference between creating value and actually getting the benefit of it.

And I think there’s something very human going on here. We get excited about what something could do. We want to believe it’ll work. We’ve got a problem to fix, people expecting answers and a decision to make. So we make the decision.

But did we really think through what would have to change afterwards? Who was going to make sure those hours were put to good use? What would people stop doing? What would get in their way? What would we do if things weren’t working out as expected? And how soon would we notice?

I’ve seen too many businesses spend more money trying to fix disappointing results without first finding out why the last investment didn’t deliver.

Sometimes you made a perfectly sensible decision with the information you had. Then things changed. Or something you were counting on didn’t happen. That’s life.

What matters is how quickly you notice, whether you’re prepared to question what you believed, and what you can still do about it.

Before you spend more money trying to improve performance, wouldn’t it be worth finding out what’s actually stopping you getting the results you expected from what you’ve already paid for?
