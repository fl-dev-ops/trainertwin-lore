Your CRM measures selling better than buying

Open almost any sales dashboard and you’ll get a pretty good account of what your salespeople have been doing. Calls, emails, meetings, demos, proposals, follow-ups, next steps, stage changes and forecast updates. We can measure the hell out of selling.

Now show me what the buyer has been doing.

Who have they spoken to internally? Who got involved that wasn’t involved before? What work have they completed? What have they investigated? What have they agreed to spend? What changed their requirements? What did they say they’d do and then actually do?

Some of that will be in the CRM. Some will be buried in an email, mentioned on a call, sitting in Slack or remembered by the salesperson. Some happened inside the customer’s business and we don’t know about it at all.

That’s fine. We don’t need to know everything.
The problem starts when we treat what we don’t know as though we do.

A seller sends a proposal and the CRM records an event. They follow up three times and we get three more events. Their manager reviews the deal and we get another event. The buyer can spend the same week discussing the purchase internally, losing the support of the CFO and discovering a security problem that could kill the whole thing, and our systems may record none of it.

So activity can be going up while our knowledge of the buying decision hasn’t improved at all.
That’s what bothers me about a lot of sales data. We’re extremely good at measuring what’s easy for us to see, then we use it to make judgements about something happening somewhere else.

Surely we want to be able to put the two histories next to each other. What did the seller do? What did the buyer demonstrably do? What changed between them?

Until we can do that, I’d be reluctant about calling a busy opportunity a progressing one.
