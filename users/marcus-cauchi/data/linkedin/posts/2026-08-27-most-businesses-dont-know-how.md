Most businesses don’t know how much money they keep spending after the evidence has gone bad.

A deal stays in the forecast, people keep chasing it and managers keep talking about it. Sellers, engineers and execs are still giving it time, even though somewhere along the way the case for continuing has become much weaker than anyone wants to admit.

Nate Nasralla raised a really useful question about this. If a seller’s job is to help a customer reach a clear decision, why do we only reward the yes? Why not reward a clean no as well?

I like the question, but I’m not sure the valuable thing is the no. The valuable thing is knowing early enough what still deserves your time, money and attention.

A customer saying “no” on a call helps, but you still need to know what the no actually means. Is it no to us, no to the project, no at this price, no this quarter, or no until something else changes? And does the person saying it have enough authority for us to act on it?

That matters because once you attach money to a result, people learn how to produce the result. Pay for nos and someone will eventually get very good at getting nos. Demand 4x pipeline coverage and punish sellers when pipeline disappears, and they’ll get very good at keeping weak deals alive.

So, I wouldn’t start with the SPIF. I’d start with the money we’re wasting because we’re still treating something as real after the evidence has weakened.
How much management attention, selling time and resource are tied up in deals, plans or forecasts that no longer deserve the confidence we’re placing in them? And what changes if we get to a solid answer sooner?

I want to know whether we got people back sooner, whether the forecast got more accurate, whether we stopped spending money on dead pursuits, and whether the remaining pipeline improved. I’d also want to know how many opportunities we killed that later came back to life.

If those numbers improve, Nate’s idea has uncovered something bigger than a sales comp mechanic. He’s pointed at a cost most businesses barely measure, the money they keep spending after the evidence stopped deserving their confidence.
