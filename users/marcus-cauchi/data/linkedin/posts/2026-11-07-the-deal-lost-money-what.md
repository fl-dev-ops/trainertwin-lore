The deal lost money. What are we going to change?

Most CEOs would ask that, and they’d be right to.

The costs ended up exceeding the revenue. Delivery took longer than expected, and the customer needed far more support than anyone had allowed for.

Now the business wants to make sure it doesn’t happen again.

Do we tighten qualification, change the commission plan, introduce another approval or replace someone?

Any of those might be justified. But before changing how we work, we need to establish what actually went wrong. A disappointing result doesn’t automatically mean the original decision was poor.

We might have made a sound choice based on what we knew at the time. Perhaps circumstances changed after we signed, or the customer didn’t keep their side of the bargain. Or perhaps the warning signs were there before we signed, but nobody acted on them.

Several of these could be true at once. Until we understand what contributed to the loss, how can we know what needs fixing?

If the contract is still losing money or the customer is paying for our mistake, we may need to act now to limit the damage. We don’t have to establish every cause before doing something useful.

Preventing a repeat is a different matter.

What did we know when we signed? What could we reasonably have found out? What happened afterwards? And is this one deal, or the fifth like it?

Suppose we discover that our estimates were wrong.

Would another approval make future estimates more reliable? Or would it simply mean another person signing off the same bad information?

Knowing the cause doesn’t automatically tell us the cure.

Before making a lasting change, ask whether it would have prevented the problem or made it less likely. And what might that change cost us in time, money, lost opportunities or new problems?

If we can’t explain why the proposed change would help, and what we might lose by making it, why are we doing it?
