Richard Spanier’s post makes the sharp point: AI will not fix “good enough” sales work. It will expose it.

David Brock has been challenging that standard for years. Richard’s post shows why AI may now make it visible at scale.

I’d add this. The next constraint is not seller productivity. It is management quality.

If AI gives sellers time back, most companies will spend it the way they already spend attention: more accounts, more touches, more pipeline hygiene, and more internal reporting. Same standard. Faster production.

That is not progress. That is automation of the old model.

The harder question is who decides what the reclaimed time is for. Better selling does not appear because admin load drops. Someone has to raise the standard, protect the time, and define what better work means.

That is management work.

Can managers coach judgment, not just inspect numbers? Can they help sellers understand the buyer’s problem before the solution gets pushed? Can they test for real buyer movement, and defend value when finance, procurement, or risk enter the conversation?

Can they tell when a forecast is built on evidence, not hope?

This is where AI may expose the real gap. It may expose management before it exposes sellers.

Many sales managers were promoted because they could sell, or because they could enforce process. Far fewer were taught how to develop commercial judgment in others.

That matters now. When routine work recedes, what remains is the work buyers notice: problem framing, decision support, value defence, risk reduction, consensus building, and continuity from first conversation to outcome.

AI can remove friction. It cannot decide what good looks like.

Leadership has a choice. Use the time to increase volume, or use it to improve the economics of growth: higher win rates, cleaner forecasts, shorter cycles, stronger expansion, and less waste.

AI doesn't make the work better. It makes the standard impossible to hide.
