You’ve asked the sales team to increase activity. Calls and meetings are up, and the CRM shows more opportunities. Revenue hasn’t improved enough, so management is considering another intervention.

More coaching? A revised process? Different targets? Replacing people who haven’t improved?

Any of those might help. But the first intervention has already changed what people do, without establishing why the intended outcome hasn’t followed.
Perhaps the salespeople need more time. Perhaps they’re struggling with unsuitable opportunities. Perhaps the problem lies elsewhere.
What makes you confident the next intervention will address the cause?

If the explanation is wrong, the business could pay for more activity, more management and another attempted remedy without getting closer to the result it needs.

That’s what I examine in When Did We Decide That Worked?: how an apparent improvement can justify another investment before we’ve established whether the first intervention achieved what we intended.
