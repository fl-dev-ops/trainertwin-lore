The first miss made sense. Two hires were late, one deal moved and the market was softer than anyone expected.

The second miss was harder to explain, but still recoverable. By the third, the explanation had not changed. It just needed more caveats each time.
That is usually the point where the decision needs to come back. The business may still have an execution problem, but there are now at least four possibilities worth separating.

The team may be capable but working against a plan the market no longer supports. The plan may still be sound, but the company lacks the authority or resources to execute it. Management may be avoiding a difficult truth. Or the investment case may have assumed commercial capability that was never there.

The next decision is not simply whether to keep believing or replace the team.
It is whether to proceed, narrow the plan, change the conditions, gather specific evidence or return the whole decision to the board.

The frequently unasked question is:
What new evidence justifies another quarter of relying on the same explanation?
