I’ve been that seller.

One of those deals was when I was selling software to Argos. The buyer used to bring other people into the conversations. We’d agreed the next steps, they were asking sensible questions and it felt as though we were moving towards a decision. Then the meetings started slipping, fewer people were involved and the harder commercial questions stopped getting answered.

I knew what that usually meant. The deal wasn’t as strong as it had been, even though nothing in the CRM had changed.

My manager could see it too, but the opportunity was already in the forecast. Taking it out created a problem, so we agreed to leave it where it was for another week and try one more email, one more call and one more route into the account.

Neither of us was being dishonest. I didn’t want to give up too soon, my manager didn’t want to overreact and the business still needed to plan around the number.

The trouble was that the forecast was now based on what the buyer had been doing a few weeks earlier, not what they were doing then. By the next review, we were still talking about the same stage, close date and value, even though the deal itself had moved backwards.

This is how forecasts become unreliable. It’s rarely one big lie or one terrible decision. More often, it’s a reasonable decision that nobody revisits when the evidence changes.

I’ve spent more than thirty years in sales, and this is one of the things the job keeps teaching me. Buyers are free to change their minds, lose urgency, involve different people or stop the process altogether. Our systems are much slower to admit that.

Before your next forecast call, look at the deals you’re counting on and ask a simple question. What has the buyer done recently that still justifies the stage, close date and confidence you’ve given it?

There’s probably at least one deal in there that’s being judged on old evidence.
