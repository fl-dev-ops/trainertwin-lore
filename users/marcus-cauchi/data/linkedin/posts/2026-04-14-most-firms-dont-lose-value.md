Most firms don't lose value because the contract was weak. They lose value because the structure can't survive once delivery begins.

That usually starts in familiar places:

Assumptions that were never tested hard enough
A handover that transfers paperwork but not judgment
Trust built in the pursuit and lost in the transition
Capability presented as if it were already a dependable service
Risk priced in but left invisible
Functions buying toward different outcomes
Governance arriving after the value has already started leaking

This is why so many supplier arrangements disappoint.

It's also why so many AI implementations are becoming expensive faster than expected.

The pattern is the same. Leaders treat execution risk as an operating issue when it is really a design issue. By the time it shows up in delivery, margin is thinner, decisions are slower, management attention is diverted, and trust has already started to erode.

The better question is not whether the deal looked robust at signing.

It is whether the business preserved enough clarity, ownership, and decision quality to keep the economics intact when conditions changed.

That is where the real cost sits:
- rework,
- delay,
- escalation,
- management drag,
and lost confidence in the system itself.

The cheapest option often becomes the most expensive for one reason: the organisation bought an answer before it had designed how the answer would survive contact with reality.

Question for CEOs, operating partners, and board members:

Where does your organisation first lose control of the economics between commercial promise and live execution?
