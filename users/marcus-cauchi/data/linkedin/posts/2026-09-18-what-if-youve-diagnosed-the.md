What if you’ve diagnosed the wrong SDR problem?

The problem with deciding your SDRs aren’t making enough calls isn’t just that you might be wrong. It’s what you do next.

If you think it’s an effort problem, you push harder. But if it’s actually poor data, capability, account understanding or a target that was never tested against the work required, more pressure doesn’t fix it. It can make it worse.

Good SDRs can start looking slow because they’re checking what’s true before they call. Someone else can look productive because they’re getting through records faster. The dashboard can’t necessarily tell the difference.

So managers can end up coaching the wrong problem. They can tell someone who needs better information to work faster, tell someone who needs to understand the customer to research less, or judge somebody’s performance without knowing whether the work was comparable. And if someone really is avoiding the phone, you can waste weeks fixing everything except the thing that actually needs fixing.

Meanwhile, prospects get irrelevant calls. People who’ve already said no get approached again. SDRs have worse conversations. Poor information gets passed to the next person, and the CRM becomes harder to trust.

Then the same data comes back around as evidence for the next management decision. Eventually, you can have a sales team making more calls from information nobody should have trusted, while the dashboard tells management performance is improving.

Now you can’t reliably tell good work from fast work, an effort problem from a capability problem, or individual underperformance from a problem created by the system. Get the cause wrong and the action you take can make the real problem worse.

Is Call Avoidance Manufactured by Management? starts with a simple example: an SDR makes 47 calls against a target of 100.
Before you tell them to make more calls, what does that number actually tell you about why they didn’t?
