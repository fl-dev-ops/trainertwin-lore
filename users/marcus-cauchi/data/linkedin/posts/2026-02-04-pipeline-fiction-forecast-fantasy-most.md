Pipeline Fiction = Forecast Fantasy

Most pipeline problems are not caused by weak sellers or poor qualification.
They are caused by systems that allow fiction to enter quietly and reward it for staying plausible.

If you have ever defended a forecast you did not fully trust, you already know this cost.
Not just to the business, but to your credibility. To your time. To the conversations you keep having every quarter that feel more like negotiation than reporting.

Deals do not become fictional when they stall.

They become fictional earlier, when confidence replaces evidence and activity is mistaken for progress.
A meeting accepted is not demand.
A champion is not momentum.
A deal that cannot survive your absence is not a deal.
Most teams sense this and tolerate it. They call it optimism. They call it coverage. They call it managing risk.

What they are doing is allowing narrative to occupy space where proof should exist.

Here is a simple test you can run before your next forecast call.
Select the deal you are most confident will close this quarter.
Answer each question Yes or No.
Mostly is No. We are working on it is No.

Has the buyer told you ...
Has the buyer named a specific ...
Is there a meeting on the calendar that the buyer ...
Have you seen written proof that ...
If you stopped all contact today ...

Click the link to learn the questions:

Five Yes answers does not mean the deal will close.
It means it exists without you sustaining it.
Anything less means you are carrying it.

If your forecast contains deals that only exist because you keep pushing them forward, your forecast is not a prediction. It is a position.

This is not a judgement of effort or intent. Good sellers produce fiction all the time when the system rewards continuity over truth.

If this feels familiar, it is not because you are failing.
It is because your pipeline has no mechanism to prevent fiction from entering in the first place.

Better judgement will not fix that.
Better questions will not fix that.
Only architecture does.

The uncomfortable part is not seeing this.
It is deciding whether you will keep paying for what you can now clearly see.
