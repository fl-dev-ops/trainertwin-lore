Is your CRM making weak evidence look certain?
 
“They want to do something this year” becomes “Q4 decision.”
“They said this is costing them £500k” becomes “£500k business case.”
“The CFO needs to be involved” becomes “economic buyer identified.”
“They liked what they saw” becomes “strong customer interest.”
 
Nobody has lied. Nobody needs to exaggerate anything. But somewhere between the customer conversation and the forecast call, what the customer actually said becomes what the business believes.
 
Each person inherits the confidence of the person before them, but they don’t inherit the uncertainty that came with the original evidence.
 
CRMs record conclusions: stage, probability, amount, close date, next step. They don’t show the person reading those fields what the customer actually said or did that justified the conclusion.
 
So “Q4 decision” can sit there without the other half of the sentence: “but there are three other projects competing for the money.” The £500k can become a business case even though it started as somebody’s rough estimate. The CFO can appear as the economic buyer because somebody said she would need to be involved, even though nobody has spoken to her yet.
 
None of those clues is worthless. The problem starts when the clue gets separated from its source, loses its uncertainty and then gets repeated as fact.
 
The opportunity progresses. The stage changes. A probability gets attached to it. It turns up in forecast calls. Managers start planning around it. By then, the original conversation may be several people away, but the confidence has survived the journey better than the caveats.
 
You can fix that without buying anything. Put the customer evidence next to the interpretation.
 
If the CRM says “Q4 decision”, record what the customer actually said or did that supports Q4. If it says “£500k business case”, keep the source of the £500k with it. If the confidence changes, keep the evidence that changed it visible too.
 
That lets somebody who wasn’t on the call see both what you believe and why you believe it. It still doesn’t mean the evidence is strong enough. Good notes can preserve weak evidence perfectly well.
 
So run the Favourite Deal Test.
 
Take the opportunity everyone feels best about and ask two questions. What evidence supports the confidence we currently have in this deal? And what would have to be true for us to downgrade it today?
 
If the evidence doesn’t justify the confidence, reduce the confidence to what the evidence supports.
 
Then write down what you would need to learn before raising it again.
 
A stage shouldn’t protect yesterday’s story from what you learned today.
