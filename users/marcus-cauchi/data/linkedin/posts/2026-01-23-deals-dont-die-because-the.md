Deals don't die because the demo was weak. They die because the demo was premature. If deals look strong after the demo and fall apart in finance, legal, or consensus, this is usually why.

Most teams think demos sell. They do not.

Demos are proof, not persuasion. Early on, buyers are not choosing a product.
They are deciding whether change is worth the risk. A demo can't help with that decision.

A demo explains how something works. Buyers are still trying to understand why anything should change.

So when demos show up too soon, the same pattern follows.

Cognitive load spikes. New language arrives before the problem is clear. Questions stop. The demo shows capability.

The buyer needs meaning.

Here's the moment most teams recognise.

The demo lands well. Everyone nods. Later, finance asks for the model. The champion cannot defend the change. The deal slows.

Nothing went wrong in the demo. Confusion entered the system. This is the inversion most teams miss.

Demos validate. They do not persuade.
Conviction comes from clarity.

Clarity about:
* the problem.
* the cost of doing nothing.
* what success looks like.
* why this approach makes sense now.

Only then does a demo help. And only if you change what it is for.

Stop using demos as presentations. Use them as tests. Not here is what we do. But let us see if what you think you need actually holds up.

That protects the buyer. They can ask naive questions. They can surface misfit before they lose political capital. They leave with language they can defend.

It protects the seller too. False momentum shows up fast. Unseen stakeholders surface. Bad deals stop before they poison the forecast.

If demos increase excitement but weaken consensus, the issue is not execution.
It is timing.

The next move is not a better demo. It's earning clarity before you show anything.
