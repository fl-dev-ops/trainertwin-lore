When a PE-owned business falls behind plan, how do you know whether it needs a different leader or whether the leader is being asked to deliver something the business can no longer afford?

Liam Grier’s latest article raises some interesting questions about what private equity investors now expect from the people they appoint to run and transform their portfolio companies.

But there’s an economic tension worth examining.

PE investors can no longer rely as heavily on cheap borrowing and rising valuations to generate returns. More of the value has to come from improving the businesses they buy.
At the same time, those businesses may be servicing substantial acquisition debt, paying fees and making distributions to their owners.

Depending on the deal, those commitments can leave less money to develop people, improve operations, win customers and build the capacity needed to grow.

What happens when the business can’t afford to do all of those things?
The investment plan may have made sense when the deal was signed. But borrowing costs change, customers spend less, margins tighten and growth takes longer or costs more than expected.
The business falls behind plan.
The board considers replacing the CEO, appointing a transformation executive, cutting costs or investing in technology.
Any of those decisions might be right.

But there’s an assumption worth challenging.

That the performance gap is something management can close without changing the financial commitments, resources or expectations placed on the business.

What if the people running it are capable, but the numbers no longer work together?
Equally, what if the plan is achievable and management isn’t delivering?

Those are different problems. They call for different decisions.

Before spending more money, wouldn’t you want to establish which assumptions still hold, what’s actually preventing performance and what the proposed intervention can realistically change?

Otherwise, you could replace a capable executive, lose valuable time and leave the underlying problem untouched. Or retain someone who isn’t delivering because you’ve mistaken poor execution for an impossible plan.

Liam, when clients ask you to find someone to turn performance around, how often is the brief clear about what’s preventing performance and what the new executive will have the authority to change?
