What I Learned About Myself from a 15-Minute Sales Self-Check

I’ll be honest: I don’t usually do “self-assessments” (they tend to feel either too fluffy or like a performance for my boss). But last week, I tried something different, the Ally Method™ Value & Money Self-Check. And it actually stuck with me.

I’m not a top 1% sales rep. I hit quota some quarters, sometimes just under, sometimes just over. I care about my clients, I want to close business, but I know I have patterns, especially when it comes to price conversations.

What surprised me about this self-check was how direct it was. There’s no “right answer.” No score. Just questions like, “Would you rather lose a deal at full price, or win with a discount?” and “When are you most likely to give extras?” And then: “Who will you actually tell about the commitment you make?”

Taking 15 minutes to be totally honest, no manager watching, no pressure to impress, showed me I give in way more often than I’d like, especially at the end of the quarter. I also realized my real motivator isn’t just closing the deal, but avoiding conflict. (Ouch.)

But here’s what’s different: I picked one thing to pause and sit with he silence before I respond to any pricing objection. And I told my friend in the team (the self-check makes you write the name down). I put a calendar reminder to check back in a week.

I don’t expect to turn into a “price integrity” superhero overnight. But I do feel more aware. I can see my habits a little more clearly. And I’m not going to beat myself up, just notice and nudge myself forward.

I asked Marcus to interpret my responses ... and he wasn't as scary as I thought he'd be. I left with a bunch of usable insights and have referred him to my boss because our team has an average discount rate of 32% when we are meant to cap at 20%. Our results this year have seen people burnout, leave or put on PIPs which they never survive. What a waste of human potential. 

If you’re like me, solid but not perfect, wanting to get better, tired of all-or-nothing pressure, try this tool. Sometimes the best progress comes from small honest steps, not huge leaps.
