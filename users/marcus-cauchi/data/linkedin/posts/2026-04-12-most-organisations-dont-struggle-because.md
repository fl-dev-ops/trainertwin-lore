Most organisations don’t struggle because people aren’t working hard. They struggle because the signals used to judge performance are misleading.

You see it in leadership selection. Someone performs well in visible settings, communicates with confidence, and handles senior stakeholders comfortably. That gets read as readiness. But once they step into the role, the effect on the team is different. Fewer decisions get made without escalation, challenge reduces, and more judgement sits with the manager. Nothing looks obviously broken, but the team is less able to operate independently.

The same pattern shows up in commercial systems. Activity looks strong, pipeline is active, and deals are closing. But beyond the first sale, the structure weakens. A smaller proportion of revenue comes from expansion, accounts do not deepen consistently, and growth depends on continually adding new deals rather than building on what exists.

In both cases, the system is rewarding signals that are easy to observe rather than outcomes that compound.

That has a cost. You start adding managers who increase coordination faster than they increase team output, and revenue that requires constant replacement rather than generating follow-on growth. More resource is required just to maintain the same level of performance.

Once a team defaults to escalation over judgement, and revenue depends on replacement rather than expansion, the system loses its ability to scale. Growth becomes a function of input, not strength.

At that point, this is no longer a leadership or sales issue. Capital is being allocated to activity and structure that do not increase underlying performance.

Question for CEOs and operating partners:

Where in your organisation are you investing in management and revenue activity that cannot operate without continued intervention?
