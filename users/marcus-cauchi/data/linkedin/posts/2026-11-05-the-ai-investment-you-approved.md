The AI investment you approved may have been the right decision.

But is it still the decision your business is relying on today?

The pilot worked. People adopted it. Productivity improved. Then things changed. Processes were redesigned. Staffing changed. Contracts deepened. The old ways of working became harder to restore. The business may still be getting genuine value.

But what does it now depend on that wasn’t part of the original decision?

Have costs actually fallen, or moved elsewhere? Has profitability improved, or are people simply producing more? And if the technology gets something important wrong, can anyone recognise it, challenge it and put things right before the consequences become unavoidable?

There’s nothing wrong with investing in AI.

The problem arises when the commitment grows beyond what the evidence and existing approval can reasonably support.

That’s the question behind my new white paper, The AI Value Evidence Gap.
It draws on research into AI economics, automation, human judgement and governance, alongside challenges and contributions from people who helped sharpen the thinking.

It’s written for CEOs, CFOs, boards and investors responsible for consequential AI decisions.

The paper examines what constitutes evidence of real business value, how dependence can grow unnoticed, and when an earlier decision needs to be reconsidered.

Sometimes the evidence will justify investing more. Sometimes it’ll justify changing course. The original decision doesn’t have to have been wrong for the decision today to be different.

And if you’ve already had to reconsider an AI investment because the business became more dependent on it than expected, I’d be interested in what changed your thinking.
