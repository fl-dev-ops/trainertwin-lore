Most sales teams don’t lose to competitors. They lose to procedure. Procurement has a system for this. PICOS. Price Improvement and Cost Optimisation Strategy. A name only an accountant could love.

It works because it’s calm, tidy, and patient. The seller brings the emotion.

Recent engagement: clean example. PICOS buyer. Seller who genuinely wanted to help, but was welded to the outcome because he was behind on quota. That’s the perfect recipe. Procurement stretches time and scope. Seller interprets silence as “momentum” because the alternative would hurt.

The buyer’s side collected clarity. The seller’s side collected hope. Hope is lighter but completely useless at quarter-end.

Nobody was rude. No threats. No cartoon villains. Just process.

Procurement got what procurement needed: numbers, options, deniability, and time. The seller got what needy sellers get: work. Work looks like progress until you notice the forecast never moves.

Founders fall for this too. They crave revenue, not validation. Warmth feels safer than accuracy, so they stay in the conversation long after the conversation has stopped being a decision.

PE sees it later in dashboards: inflated late-stage pipeline, heroic close plans, eroded pricing discipline, and a monkish faith that “buying signs” still mean something.

PICOS isn’t hostile. It’s structural. It protects jobs by slowing commitments. Quota pressure is not structural. It just makes you easy to manage.

One question would have saved months: “Who can say no to this and not get blamed?” If you don’t know the answer, you’re not in a deal. You’re in a holding pattern with good manners.

If you’ve seen this before, drop the moment you realised it wasn’t a deal. One sentence is enough.
