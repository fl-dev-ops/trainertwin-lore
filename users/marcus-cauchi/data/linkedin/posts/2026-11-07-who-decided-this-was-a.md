Who decided this was a good deal?
Sales qualified it.
Finance checked the margin.
Legal approved the terms.
Delivery agreed the timetable.
Procurement negotiated the price.
The customer approved the business case.
Everyone signed off their part.

Six months later, the supplier is losing money and the customer isn’t getting the results it expected.

Sales says the deal was properly qualified. Finance says the numbers worked when they approved it. Delivery says the work was harder than expected. The customer says it was promised more.

Each of those explanations could be true.

But there’s a question worth asking. Did anyone establish whether this was a good deal for both sides?
Not whether the paperwork was right or the budget existed.
Whether the customer was likely to get what it needed, and whether the supplier could deliver it at a cost that made the work worthwhile.
What did each side need the other to do?
What assumptions were they relying on?
Which of those had been tested?
And what happened when someone raised a concern?

Each party may have been doing its job properly while working towards a different definition of success.
Did anyone check whether those definitions could be met together?
Perhaps it was a sensible decision and circumstances changed. Perhaps the problem arose in delivery.
Or perhaps the warning signs were there and nobody joined them up.

We can’t tell without looking.
And the point of looking isn’t to find someone to blame. It’s to understand what happened and what needs to change before the next deal.
A signed contract doesn’t mean the customer made a good purchase.
A sale doesn’t mean the supplier won good business.
Both can get exactly what they agreed to and still end up worse off.

Before celebrating the next big deal, ask:
What needs to be true for both sides to be glad they signed it?
