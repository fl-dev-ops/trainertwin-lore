Why Waste Starts Earlier Than You Think

When results slow down, most organisations look at the teams. They review execution, question capability, and add oversight. It feels responsible. It also misses the point more often than we admit.

Waste usually begins earlier, in the decisions that shape the work before anyone starts doing it. Leaders choose how many priorities are active at once. They decide which metrics affect pay and promotion. They add approval steps to reduce risk. None of that looks dramatic in isolation. Over time, it defines how people actually behave.

If eight initiatives are labeled urgent, attention splits. Work moves in short bursts. Projects stall, then reappear under a new heading. The activity is real. The progress is not what it should be.

If collaboration is required but incentives stay individual, people protect their own targets. They are not being difficult. They are responding to what the system rewards.

If a routine decision needs three or four approvals, delays are not surprising. The friction was built in the moment the extra layer was added.

You can see the signs. Teams spend more time preparing updates than resolving blockers. Internal metrics are met while external results lag. Meetings grow because clarity was not settled at the start.

Most leaders add priorities faster than they remove them. Each one makes sense at the time. Few get fully retired. After a while, the organization is carrying more than it can finish.

None of these conditions appear on their own. Someone approved them. Someone agreed they were necessary. That is how they became normal.

Before questioning effort, look at the design.

Count how many initiatives are currently described as critical. If the list is long, that is not an execution problem. It is a choice.

The system is not confused. It is responding to the instructions it has been given.
