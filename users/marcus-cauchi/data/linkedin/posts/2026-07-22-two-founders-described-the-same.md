Two founders described the same frustration.

They'd explained what they wanted. They'd put expectations in writing. Yet capable people were still waiting for them to decide, approve or step in.

At first, that looks like a weak-team problem. It may be a problem the founder is helping to reproduce. The work had been delegated. The authority had not.

In both businesses, people were allowed to act, but only until the founder reinterpreted the priority, challenged the timing or redirected the work. Each intervention made sense on its own. Together, they taught the same lesson: your decision is provisional until I confirm it.

That creates a damaging loop.

The founder sees hesitation and intervenes. Repeated intervention makes checking the safer option. Decisions travel upwards. Managers become messengers. Work slows. The founder inherits responsibilities they believed they had delegated.

Then the founder concludes that nobody takes ownership. This is learned waiting. It is not always caused by fear, laziness or lack of care. It can be the rational response to authority that disappears when tested.

Before asking people to take more ownership, pick one decision that keeps returning to you.

Define what they can decide, what requires consultation, what requires approval and whether you will allow a reasonable decision to stand when you would have chosen differently.

Ownership grows when authority survives first contact with the founder.
