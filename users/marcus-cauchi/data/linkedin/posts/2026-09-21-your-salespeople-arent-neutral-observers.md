Your salespeople aren’t neutral observers of your pipeline

We ask salespeople to give us an accurate picture of the pipeline. Fair enough. Someone has to tell us what’s happening.

Then we give them a pipeline target, coverage target, activity target, stage requirements, forecast category and quota, and use the data they provide to decide whether they’re performing.

That’s a strange measurement system when you look at it closely.

It doesn’t mean salespeople are dishonest. Most aren’t sitting there inventing opportunities or deliberately falsifying close dates. You don’t need any of that for the data to become distorted.

You just need some answers to be easier to give than others.

Imagine you’ve got £2 million of pipeline and your manager expects £3 million. You’ve just had a call that makes you much less confident about a £400,000 deal.
You could say, “I don’t know enough yet.”
You could move the close date.
You could reduce the probability.
You could take it out of the forecast.
You could close it.

Each of those creates a different conversation with your manager.

Or you can leave it alone until you know more.
That’s where this gets interesting. If changing the record when evidence weakens creates more immediate pain than leaving it alone, what behaviour should we expect the system to produce over time?

Not because people are bad. Because people learn.
They learn what gets challenged. They learn which answers produce another twenty minutes of questioning. They learn what happens when coverage falls. They learn whether management genuinely values early bad news or merely says it does.

Then leadership looks at the CRM and asks why the data isn’t clean enough.
Don’t start with CRM hygiene.
I’d start by asking what happens to someone who changes a commercially important number downwards because the evidence changed.
If the answer is that they immediately have to justify themselves, rescue the deal and replace the number, we’ve learned something about the data we’re going to get.
