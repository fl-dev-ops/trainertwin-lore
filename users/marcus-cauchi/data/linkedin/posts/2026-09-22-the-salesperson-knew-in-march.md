The salesperson knew in March. The plan caught up in May.

Say a customer tells the salesperson in early March that their budget has been pushed into next year. The salesperson updates the CRM. Their manager accepts it at the next pipeline review, and the CRO takes £500,000 out of the forecast. So far, so good.

Finance reviews the operating plan in May, on its normal cycle. Nobody has necessarily done anything wrong. The salesperson updated what they knew, the manager accepted it, the CRO changed the forecast, and Finance reviewed the plan when it always does. Each step was reasonable on its own terms.

Yet for several weeks, one part of the business knew the £500,000 wasn't coming while other parts could still have been hiring, spending and budgeting as if it was.

Maybe that didn't matter. The hires might have gone ahead anyway, and the spending might have been justified without the £500,000. Or it might have mattered a great deal. From the outside you can't tell, and my read is that many businesses can't tell from the inside either.
We're reasonably good at asking how quickly bad news reaches the forecast. I'm less sure we ask how quickly it reaches everything that was relying on the old number.

Think about the last time something significant came out of your forecast. Note when someone first knew, when the forecast changed, and when the people making hiring, spending and budget decisions found out. Then look at what was committed in between.

How much of it would you still have done if everyone had known what the salesperson knew?
