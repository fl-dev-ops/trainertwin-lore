When deals feel good and then stop moving, it’s almost never because the buyer lost interest.

It’s because someone looked at what comes next and decided it wasn’t safe yet.

That hesitation usually doesn’t get explained. It just shows up as delay. Or politeness. Or “let me take this back internally”. There are a few things I listen for in those moments.

The first is the room I’m not in.

So I’ll ask something like, “Okay, what happens to this after we finish here?”

And if I need to be clearer, “Where does this go next, and who’s in that room?”

That tells me pretty quickly whether this is going somewhere real, or whether my contact is just enjoying the conversation. Because the meeting that matters is usually the next one. And it has different rules.

The second thing I pay attention to is whether the idea survives without me.

If it only makes sense when I’m explaining it, then it doesn’t really exist yet. So before we wrap, I might say, “Can I check something? If you had to explain this to your CFO or Head of Ops, how would you describe it?”

Then I shut up.

If it comes back clean, great. If it rambles, or leans on slides, or needs rescuing, nothing is stuck. It’s just not ready to travel. And no amount of follow-up will change that.

The third thing is the human outcome.

Companies don’t take risks. People do.

Someone has to walk into that next room and put their name next to this.
They only do that if the personal maths works. So I’ll ask, “If this works, who’s actually better off inside the organisation?”

Sometimes I’ll make it more blunt: “Who looks good if this goes right, and who gets nervous if it doesn’t?”

If the answer is “the company”, that’s usually a sign we’re early. It doesn’t mean no. It just means the path isn’t safe yet.

Those three moments tell me most of what I need to know.

They show me how decisions really get made. They show me where the risk actually sits. And they tell me whether this idea can be carried forward without hurting someone.

And once you see that, the urge to push tends to disappear, because in complex deals, momentum isn’t about speed. It’s about whether the idea can move into the next room, carried by someone who won’t regret doing it.

Everything else is just noise.
