  For any public-private deal, I’d want to establish before arguing about who deserves what: was the long-term obligation properly provided for before the return was taken or the money was spent?

  Then the harder test.
  Would we have agreed this split before we knew who was going to win?

  Before deciding who deserves the upside, find out who really owns the downside.

A lot of the argument about privatisation gets stuck on ownership. The public paid for the asset, so the public should own it. Private investors put up capital and took the risk, so they should keep the return. Neither position tells us who ultimately owns the downside.
 
Water shows why that matters. Over 2020-25, the sector’s average return on regulated equity was 1.71% against an average base return of 4.10%. Yet shareholder returns were much stronger, with Ofwat attributing much of that to inflation increasing the regulatory value of the assets rather than to management performance.
 
That distinction matters more than the usual argument about whether dividends were too high. Investors should earn a return for taking risk and putting capital to productive use. The harder question is whether money being distributed was really surplus, or whether part of the return was arriving while a foreseeable obligation remained unfunded.
 
That obligation is now hard to miss. Ofwat’s latest settlement supports £104 billion of spending between 2025 and 2030, including a huge increase in new infrastructure investment. The sector declared £645 million of dividends in 2024-25, down from £1 billion the previous year, while several companies were unable to pay because of financial restrictions or lack of distributable reserves.
 
An investor can receive a return, sell their stake and leave. Customers still need water. If the network needs repairing ten years later, somebody still has to repair it.
 
So who could leave, and who was still there when the bill arrived?
 
The test can’t use hindsight. It has to use what a competent decision-maker could reasonably have known when the money was distributed. What would the return have looked like if the foreseeable cost of maintaining the service had been properly provided for first?
 
The picture isn’t one-sided. Nearly £5 billion of new equity went into regulated water companies during 2020-25, with more committed since. Southern has agreed up to £1.2 billion from its majority investor and no dividends until 2030. A business that can never offer investors a return will eventually struggle to attract the capital its customers need.
 
Government should face the same test. It can spend today and leave future taxpayers with debt, maintenance and services that still have to be funded. Politicians can leave office while the obligation remains. The assumptions used to value those future obligations, especially inflation, time and the discount rate, need to be visible too.
 
The terms matter as much as the ownership. A bargain agreed when both sides understood the risks is different from one where the terms change after commitment, or where one party can take its return while another has lost the ability to leave.
