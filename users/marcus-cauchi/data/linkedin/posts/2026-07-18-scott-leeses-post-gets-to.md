@Scott Leese's post gets to the part most people will miss. Everyone will share the alleged 32x ROI headline, while almost nobody will pay attention to how Vercel earned the right to automate in the first place. They did not start with AI. They started by understanding the work.

They paired engineering with data science and the best subject-matter expert they had. Then they ran the agent alongside their best SDR for six weeks, reviewing and improving its work until the human could no longer materially improve the output. That is not a clever prompt or a tool implementation. It is disciplined observation, workflow design and supervised testing.

It also raises the question that comes after a successful shadow period: what happens once the human leaves? The market changes, customer behaviour changes and the business changes. Exceptions emerge, people move on and the knowledge that made the process work begins to thin out.

The AI does not have to fail for the business to become more exposed. Reality only has to move beyond the conditions under which the automation originally proved itself. The system may continue producing acceptable outputs while the assumptions, judgement and recovery capability behind it become progressively less reliable.

That is why governance cannot stop at deployment. The organisation has to preserve the ability to recognise when the original assumptions are no longer true, understand what has changed, and know who has the authority to intervene before small changes become expensive failures. Watching the outputs is not enough when the conditions that gave those outputs meaning are changing.

The six-week shadow period proved that the agent could do the work under the conditions in which it was tested. The next challenge is ensuring the organisation can still recognise when it should not. That includes preserving enough expertise to question the system, recover the process and act before dependence removes the remaining alternatives.

Scott’s post is a powerful reminder that AI does not replace good operating discipline. It amplifies whatever discipline, judgement and governance already exist. The organisations that benefit most will not simply automate faster. They will remain capable of knowing when yesterday’s successful automation is no longer safe to trust.
