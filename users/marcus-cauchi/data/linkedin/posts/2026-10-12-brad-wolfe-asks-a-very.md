Brad Wolfe asks a very good question about AI governance: what’s the cheapest way to improve the number without improving anything real? 

Let’s say we fix that. The measures are sound and the AI really is making better decisions. What does that give us good reason to do next? We can be more confident about the job we’ve tested it doing. 

The problem comes when that confidence starts doing other work. We give the AI more to do, let it make bigger decisions, check it less often or become more dependent on it. Those might all be perfectly sensible decisions, but the evidence that supported the first decision doesn’t automatically settle the next one. 

Say an AI has done the same job well thousands of times. Then we give it different data, connect it to another system, let it act instead of recommend, remove the person who used to approve its decisions, or start using it where getting things wrong would do much more harm. Its track record still matters. 

The question is how much of it still applies to what we’re asking the AI to do now. We need to ask that because the job, the consequences or the safeguards may have changed. There’s another problem that worries me more. 

When something works well, relying on it more can be a perfectly sensible decision. Over time, people may check less, old ways of doing the work disappear, skills fade and keeping an alternative starts to look like wasted money. Nobody has to be careless or stupid for that to happen. Then you can end up in a strange place.

The AI is getting better and your confidence in it is based on real evidence. But if it does fail badly, you may be less able to cope without it than you were when you started. 

That’s why I think Brad’s question needs another one beside it. When the evidence gets better, what are we letting that change? 

Being more confident that something works isn’t the same decision as deciding how much we’ll let it do, how closely we’ll watch it, or how dependent we’re prepared to become. Each time we change one of those, I want to know what we’ve learned that gives us good reason to do it, and what would make us change our minds. 

The danger isn’t trusting something that works. It’s allowing good evidence about one decision to answer the next one for us. That’s where I think earned confidence can turn into unearned permission without anyone noticing.
