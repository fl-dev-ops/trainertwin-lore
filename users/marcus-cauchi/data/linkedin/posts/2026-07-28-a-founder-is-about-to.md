A founder is about to sign a £60,000 consulting engagement. The proposal is convincing, the adviser has strong references, and the plan promises faster growth, better management and a commercial system the team can use.

The accepted explanation is straightforward. The business lacks expertise, so the consultant supplies it. That may be exactly what is needed.

What rarely gets examined is where the capability will exist when the engagement ends. Will the team be able to diagnose the problem, make the decisions and maintain the improvement without the consultant, or will the business become dependent on the person hired to reduce its dependency?

That distinction becomes expensive later. The work may produce visible progress while the consultant is present, but once they leave, decisions slow down, standards weaken and the founder discovers that the capability was rented rather than built.

Consultants are not inherently unsafe. Some problems genuinely require specialist help, and short-term dependency may be reasonable during a crisis or transition.

The useful test is:
What will this business be able to do without them that it cannot do today?
A proposal that cannot answer that question may still describe useful activity. It has not yet demonstrated capability transfer.

Where the commitment is material, examine that before signing. The cost of asking is small. The cost of discovering the answer afterwards may not be.
