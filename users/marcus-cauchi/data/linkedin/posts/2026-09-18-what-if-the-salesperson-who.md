What if the salesperson who kills more deals is actually better?

Imagine two salespeople get 100 plausible opportunities each.
The first keeps 65 alive for months. They’re busy. Lots of calls, meetings, follow-ups, proposals and pipeline reviews. Eventually they close 15.
The second gets rid of 65 much earlier. Some can’t buy. Some don’t care enough. Some have other priorities. Some won’t involve the people who need to be involved. Some discover things that make continuing a bad use of everyone’s time.
They also close 15.

Which salesperson performed better?

I don’t know from those numbers alone. I’d want to know much more about the opportunities, values, customers, territories and what each salesperson actually knew when they made their decisions.

But I certainly wouldn’t assume the person with more pipeline was better.

The second salesperson may have done something commercially valuable that we’re poor at measuring. They discovered reality earlier.
That matters because dead and weak opportunities aren’t free.

People call them. Managers review them. Solution engineers support them. Executives get dragged into them. Forecasts include them. Marketing celebrates pipeline creation. Finance starts relying on some of the revenue.
Every extra week we keep one alive has a cost.

Yet if our management system rewards pipeline creation much more visibly than good pipeline removal, we’re sending a potentially dangerous signal.
“Finding an opportunity” looks productive.
Finding out that the opportunity shouldn’t exist can look like failure.

Don't we want to know how much sales capacity is currently being consumed by opportunities that the available buyer evidence stopped supporting weeks or months ago?
That number could be ugly.
