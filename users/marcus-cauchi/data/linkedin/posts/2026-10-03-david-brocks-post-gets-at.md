David Brock’s post gets at a problem that’s easy to miss.

A playbook can work so well that people keep following it after the conditions that made it useful have changed.

That’s when judgement really matters. First, you have to notice that the rule no longer fits.

But telling people to “use your judgement” doesn’t solve much on its own.

Breaking the rule isn’t proof of good judgement.

Sometimes someone has spotted something the process missed. Sometimes they’ve simply made a bad call.

And the result won’t always tell you which.

A good decision can go wrong. A bad one can get lucky.

So if we wait for the result before deciding whether the decision was sound, we risk rewarding luck and punishing good judgement.

The useful questions come earlier.

What did you know?

What were you assuming?

What didn’t fit?

What could happen if you were wrong?

What would make you stop, change course or ask for help?

You can then compare what was known at the time with what happened next.

Was the decision poor?

Was the rule wrong?

Or did a sound decision simply end badly?

That changes what we should expect from a good playbook too.

It shouldn’t just tell people what to do.

It should tell them when the rule applies, what it depends on, what should make them question it, and when the rule needs to be questioned or set aside.

Otherwise people keep following a rule after it’s stopped helping.

Or they ignore the rule and call that judgement.

The question I keep coming back to is this:

How do you tell, before you know the outcome, whether someone is using good judgement or just ignoring a good rule?
