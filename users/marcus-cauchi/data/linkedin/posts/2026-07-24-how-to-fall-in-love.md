How to Fall in Love with Sales
A true story about what happens when a top performer stops lying to close deals.

Stephen Lowe had hit his number
That should have felt like success. Instead, he was exhausted, fed up, and unsure whether he could do it again

The problem wasn't pressure. It was how he'd been taught to create movement.
"Pricing deadlines. Withholding information. Telling people prices would change if they didn't sign by a certain date. Even threatening to turn off functionality." None of it was true.

"There was just a lot of lying and manipulating." He could pressure customers to act. He didn't feel proud of how. He'd sold business he wasn't confident would expand. He knew some customers would leave. He couldn't see how many more times he could keep "holding people over a barrel" before the whole thing stopped working

The question troubling him was bigger than technique

"Is there any other way to do this where I don't feel as exhausted?" Then the real question beneath it: "Is there a way I can work honestly and still be successful, and get more meaning and purpose out of my work?"

That's the question most sellers avoid. They want better scripts, sharper qualification, new ways to apply pressure. They rarely ask whether they can live comfortably inside the method they're taught

So Stephen went back to basics: buyer safety, buyer risk, and the difference between helping someone decide and trying to exert your will over them.
He stopped forcing five deals into a quarter because the forecast needed them. He got comfortable saying: "That's this quarter, that's later this year, that's next year"

His pipeline got leaner. His forecasts got more honest. Buyers started giving him access they'd never given a seller before

In one deal, a single relationship with a CIO opened into conversations with the CFO, the managing partner, the head of innovation, and other senior leaders. Stephen went on to close his company's first deal in a brand new sector, worth mid six figures annually, over several years.

That wasn't the moment he fell in love with sales. It was proof of something bigger: he could be honest, transparent, and useful to the customer without sacrificing commercial success.

The work, "loosened my grip on what I felt I was trying to make people do, and just brought honesty into it." He'd expected tactical improvements. What he found was "a baseline philosophy that's an easier head to live with."
He still gets stressed. He still gets things wrong. He's not claiming to have become morally immaculate

But he now has a way of selling he can respect. Most founders don't hate sales. They hate believing that success requires becoming someone they can't look in the eye
