The Fortune 500 may be deleting the CMO title, but the dangerous mistake is assuming the job disappeared with it. Marketing has often been reduced to campaigns, content, leads, media and brand, while customers judge the company through sales, product, service, finance, operations, customer success and partners.

The customer does not care which function owns which part. They experience one company, and they feel the combined effect of every promise, delay, handover, invoice, product decision and service failure.

Sales can create trust or destroy it. Product can solve the problem or miss it. Finance can make the decision easy or painful. Operations decides whether the promise was real, customer success inherits whatever was sold, and partners extend the experience when the company is no longer there.

The article is right about the title. It is also right that boards want clearer accountability for growth. But replacing a CMO with a Chief Growth Officer, Chief Commercial Officer or a handful of VPs does not fix the problem. It can simply move the gaps.

The real question is not whether marketing deserves a bigger seat. It is whether anyone owns the integrity of the whole customer and commercial system.
Who decides which markets matter, who owns the truth about the customer, and who checks that the promise can be delivered before it is made? Who can stop a short-term revenue decision that damages trust, margin, retention or future demand? Who owns the learning when the expected result does not appear?

Delete the title without preserving that job and the costs spread. Sales works harder to replace weak demand, discounting replaces clear value, customer success repairs promises it did not make, product follows the loudest internal voice, and finance sees the damage only after it reaches revenue, margin or retention.

The CEO becomes the referee between functions chasing different numbers. The customer is left to make sense of the contradictions, repeat themselves, absorb the friction and live with the consequences of decisions made in separate departments.

I am not arguing that every customer-facing function should report to a traditional CMO. That would give more power to a role that may still be measured by campaigns, attribution and lead volume.

I am arguing that someone must own the promises, evidence, trade-offs and shared decisions that cross those functions. They do not need to run every department, but they do need enough authority to stop the company saying one thing, selling another and delivering something else.

The title is optional, but the job is not. The companies that get this right will not waste time defending the old CMO role. They will name who owns the whole commercial truth, give them the authority to act, and make every function answer to the same customer reality.
