Sales is a numbers game.

Most sales teams interpret that as seller activity. Calls. Emails. Conversations. Meetings. Demos. Opportunities. Pipeline. Conversion. Those numbers are useful. They tell you something about how your sales operation is performing.

They don't tell you whether your sellers understand the businesses they're selling to.

That's where discovery matters.

A lot of discovery is designed to establish whether an opportunity can progress. Is there a problem? Is there money? Who has authority? What's the timing? How will a decision get made? Who else is involved? What measures matter?

All useful things to know.

But you can answer every one of those questions and still know remarkably little about why the customer should spend the money.

That's the demand side of the equation.

How does this business make money? What drives revenue and margin? Where does cash get trapped? What limits capacity? Which costs will actually disappear? What happens if nothing changes? What else could they do with the same money?

And if they fix the problem, where does the financial benefit actually show up?

Take a simple example.

Two hundred employees each lose two hours a week to a manual process. Multiply the hours by their employment cost and you can produce a very impressive saving.

The arithmetic can be perfectly accurate and the business case can still be wrong.

If nobody leaves, no hiring is avoided and the released time doesn't create additional output, accelerate revenue, release cash or reduce a meaningful risk, the company hasn't suddenly banked the value shown on the spreadsheet.

A financially capable seller needs to understand that distinction.

They need to be able to follow the effect of a problem through revenue, margin, cash, capacity and risk. They need to understand what causes those numbers to change and how much of the claimed value the customer can realistically capture.

Sometimes that makes the case for buying stronger. Sometimes it makes it smaller. Sometimes it means there shouldn't be a deal.

That's discovery doing its job.

Here's a useful test for the next opportunity review.

Ask the seller how the customer makes money. Ask which number needs to change, what is causing the current result, how the proposed solution changes it and where the resulting value will actually appear.

If they can't explain that, they may have qualified an opportunity.
They haven't understood the demand.

Sales is a numbers game.

Teach sellers to understand the customer's numbers before asking them to produce more of ours.
