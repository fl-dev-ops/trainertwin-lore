Most execution failures do not begin with strategy. They begin earlier, when leadership dynamics quietly degrade decision quality long before anyone notices. By the time the problem becomes visible, the organisation already looks unpredictable from the outside.

Operating partners and general partners eventually see the same pattern across portfolios. A company looks strong on paper with a clear strategy, capable leadership, and adequate capital. Yet outcomes begin to behave unpredictably. Forecasts drift, initiatives stall, and execution slows in ways that are difficult to explain.

Most investors discover the issue only after the second missed quarter. By then the board is already forming explanations. The instinct is to question the strategy, replace leaders, fire salespeople, or redesign the operating model. New systems are introduced, technology is purchased, and sometimes AI becomes the proposed solution. These actions feel decisive, but they rarely address the underlying cause.

By the time volatility becomes visible externally, the real failure has already been underway internally for months. It begins inside the leadership system when disagreement disappears from meetings, decisions reopen repeatedly, and alignment in meetings begins to diverge from execution inside teams.

Most portfolios encounter this dynamic at some point. The difference is whether it is recognised early enough to stabilise the system before value begins leaking out of the company.
