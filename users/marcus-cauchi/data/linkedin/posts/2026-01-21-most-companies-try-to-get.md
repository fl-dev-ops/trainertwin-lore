Most companies try to get efficient the way you play Tetris.
Move pieces fast, hope for alignment, and blame reaction time when things get messy.

Avner Baruch (B.Eng')’s original article made the right point.
Most efficiency work is misdiagnosis, not weak execution.

Two additions from the trenches:

1. Start from the book of business, not the org chart.
Departments are symptoms. Customers are reality.

2. The task force exists for clarity only.
Not a shadow PMO. Not a transformation office.
Their job is to see the machine end to end and stop there.

And three extensions for when companies try to spend their way out of the stall:

First: give the task force decision rights.
If their outputs get sanitized by politics, the whole exercise collapses.

Second: make it decision safe.
If people cannot surface the ugly parts (sticky tools, sacred cow segments, founder promises), you are paying for theater.

Third: put cost to serve next to ARR and NRR.
Until that happens, efficiency means "do more with less" instead of "stop doing what doesn’t work and hurts".

You see the same farce over and over:

Growth stalls. Burn does not.
Leadership treats it as a speed problem instead of a system problem.
Then comes the raise. Not to fund strategy, but to subsidise a book of business that no longer makes sense.

That is when Tetris turns into Jenga.

Operators already know why.

If you’re wallowing in this right now, the starting point is almost never "fix Sales" or "fix Product". Nor is it “get more Funding”. 

It is: What does the book of business tell us about reality, and who is willing to write it down before we outsource the blame?
