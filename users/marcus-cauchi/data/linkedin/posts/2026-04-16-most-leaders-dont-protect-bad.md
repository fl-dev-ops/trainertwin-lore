Most leaders don’t protect bad decisions. They protect the people and systems that made them.

The incentives make that inevitable. You’re rewarded for hitting the number, keeping the narrative intact, and avoiding the conflict that comes with admitting something doesn’t hold. So the truth doesn’t get challenged early. It gets managed. Not lied about, just softened, delayed, or left unsaid.

Over time, that changes what the organisation pays attention to. The warning gets diluted, the assumption goes untested, and the person raising concern becomes harder to listen to. Meanwhile, the people most invested in the current way of doing things stay exactly where they are, because moving them would force a level of disruption most teams aren’t willing to absorb.

So the easier move is made.

The risk is passed on instead.

At that point, the decision isn’t really a decision anymore. It’s a sequence that’s already been set in motion. People can see the problem, but saying it out loud now carries a cost, and most organisations have trained their people to avoid that cost.

The deal still gets signed. The programme still goes live. The story still sounds right in the room.

But the outcome is already shaped.

You see the same pattern every time . The centre keeps the upside, and the edge absorbs the downside. When it finally breaks, it gets labelled execution, as if the failure started there.

It didn’t.

It started earlier, when the organisation chose to preserve the system instead of confronting what was wrong with it.

So the real question isn’t whether your strategy is sound. It’s whether your organisation is willing to face what it already knows.

Because if it isn’t, the truth won’t disappear.

It will just land somewhere else, with someone who has less power to push back.
