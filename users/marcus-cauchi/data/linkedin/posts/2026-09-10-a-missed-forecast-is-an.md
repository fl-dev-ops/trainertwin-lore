A missed forecast is an observation, not a diagnosis.

David Brock asked me what I thought about forecast obsession. His question took me somewhere slightly different. At quarter end, very different things can produce the same result: revenue we expected didn’t arrive.

Take three deals that were forecast to close. One should never have been treated as a real opportunity. In the second, the customer was interested, but we mistook interest for intent. The third was properly supported when it entered the forecast, then something important changed and the evidence underneath it stopped being current.

At quarter end, all three appear in the same place: the forecast missed.

There is another possibility I worry about more. People saw the evidence getting weaker, but changing the number had become more painful than leaving it alone. If reporting that a commit has weakened creates more trouble than maintaining the story for another week, the organisation has an information problem before it has a forecasting problem.

Those situations need different responses. Better opportunity admission won’t fix evidence that becomes stale. More forecast calls won’t fix an incentive to preserve the number. Better probabilities won’t help if the customer evidence was weak in the first place.

And sometimes the original judgement may have been reasonable. Something genuinely changed, and there was nothing the team could reasonably have known earlier that should have led to a different decision.

That matters because a miss tells us where the difference became visible. It doesn’t tell us whether the forecast process caused it, whether the original judgement was poor, or whether the outcome simply went against a sound decision.

So, after a material miss, I wouldn’t start by asking how to make the forecast more accurate. I’d go back through the opportunity and ask a different question:
Was there a preventable error, and if so, where did it first enter?
