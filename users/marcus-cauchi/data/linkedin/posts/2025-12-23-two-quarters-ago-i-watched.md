Two quarters ago, I watched a leadership team approve a plan they didn’t believe in. Not because it was the wrong move. Because reopening the decision would have forced a conversation about who pushed for it in the first place.

So the room settled on a familiar compromise.
“We’ll pressure test it later.”

Later is where accountability disappears.

In that moment, the decision lost authority. Not publicly. Silently.

No one owned the downside. No one carried the political cost. The decision stood, but only because no one wanted to reopen it.

When authority isn’t owned at the point of decision, pressure doesn’t disappear. It moves downstream.

Six months on, execution's slipping. Workarounds have spread. Morale is weak. Everyone can feel something is wrong, but naming it means pointing back to that earlier moment, when the decision was approved without conviction.

At that point, the issue isn’t delivery. It’s whether anyone still has the authority to change the decision.

If a recent decision fails, who in your organisation has the authority for it today, and who's carrying the political risk?

If those are different people, the decision is already carrying hidden risk.
