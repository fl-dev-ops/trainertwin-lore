Your SDR team is missing target. Are you teaching people to book meetings, or to recognise which meetings are worth booking?

To make that judgement, an SDR needs to understand more than someone’s job title and possible interest in a product.

What is that person responsible for? What are they trying to achieve or prevent?

Who depends on them? Whose cooperation do they need? What happens elsewhere in the business when something in their area changes?

And where could your product genuinely help, or create problems for someone else?

If SDRs don’t understand these things, what are they using to decide whether a conversation is worth having?

Marketing can select the right job titles. Sales leadership can provide talk tracks and targets. Managers can measure activity and demand more meetings.

Everyone can be doing what they’ve been asked to do while the business continues to underperform.

Poor customer understanding can contribute to weak conversations, poor qualification, wasted AE time and opportunities that go nowhere.

But those symptoms don’t establish the cause. Poor targeting, weak propositions, bad data and unrealistic expectations can produce similar results.

So how would you find out?

Listen to actual conversations. Ask SDRs why they approached those particular people and what they understood about their work.

Ask AEs which meetings justified their time, which became worthwhile opportunities and why others didn’t.

Ask marketing what evidence supports its targeting decisions.

Then examine what managers have actually taught and coached.

Have SDRs learned how their customers work, what people are accountable for and how their decisions affect others?

Or have they mainly learned how to describe the product, handle objections and book meetings?

Not every prospect needs extensive research. But people need enough understanding to recognise relevance, exercise judgement and know when a meeting isn’t justified.

More calls may improve reach. They won’t, by themselves, develop that judgement.

You may have a management and organisational learning problem being treated as an SDR productivity problem.

Before spending more on leads, data, technology or headcount, wouldn’t it be worth finding out?
