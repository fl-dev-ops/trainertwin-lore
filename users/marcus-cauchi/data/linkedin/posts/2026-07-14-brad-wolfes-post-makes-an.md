Brad Wolfe’s post makes an important point. Naming the person who answers for an AI decision is better than hiding behind a policy.

The comments took it further. A named owner needs the power to inspect the system, challenge it, stop it and refuse to sign. Without that, the name is there to absorb the blame after the event.

But even that does not solve the whole problem.

You can have the right person, proper authority, working controls and a clean audit trail, and still make the wrong decision. The facts may be out of date. The evidence may be thin. The threshold may have been wrong from the start.
I have seen this for years in sales forecasts. Every box is completed. The manager has approved the deal. The CRM shows green. Nobody can point to anything the buyer has done that makes the forecast safe.

The process passed. The judgement did not.

Ishaan G. made the useful distinction in the comments. Proof can show who approved the decision and whether they had the right to do so. It cannot, by itself, show that the decision was good enough.

AI makes this harder because one weak decision can be repeated before anyone notices the pattern. A discount may be within policy on every deal and still destroy margin across the quarter. Each action looks acceptable on its own.
So the question cannot stop at, “Who answers for this?”

What had to be true before we allowed the decision to become real?
