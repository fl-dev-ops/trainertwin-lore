Tomer Hochma ’s talking about pricing, but the same thing happens whenever a business is deciding whether to commit more money, people, time or reputation.

The recommendation can be well worked. The numbers have been checked, experienced people have been involved, and the logic makes sense. By the time it reaches the board or investment committee, though, some of the uncertainty has often gone missing. An estimate becomes a number in a spreadsheet. An assumption gets repeated until it sounds like a fact. A range becomes a single figure.

Nobody has to be careless for that to happen. It can happen simply because each handoff makes the case a little cleaner than the one before it. The problem is that the commitment can end up looking more certain than the evidence underneath it.

So go back to what the decision actually rests on. What do we know? What are we assuming? Which assumption would change the decision if it turned out to be wrong? What can we learn before the commitment gets bigger or harder to undo?

Being able to explain the recommendation matters. It still doesn’t tell you whether the evidence justifies the commitment.

That applies to pricing, forecasts, hiring, acquisitions, partnerships, market entry and capital allocation.

What are you about to commit to that looks more certain than the evidence really allows?
