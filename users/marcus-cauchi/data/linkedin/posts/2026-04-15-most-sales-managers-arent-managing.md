Most sales managers aren’t managing the business anymore. They’re managing what can safely be said about it.

That’s what the job becomes when leadership wants confidence, the board wants growth, and nobody wants the meeting where the truth lands with a thud.

So you look at the deal you know is soft, the customer who isn’t ready, the product issue nobody fixed, and the forecast line that should move but won’t. Then you make the same call again: do I say what I think and take the hit now, or do I leave it where it is and buy us another week?

That’s how it happens. Not one big lie. Just a long chain of smaller edits.

Leave it in commit. Don’t kill it yet. See if they can sign this quarter. Tighten the wording. Clean up the CRM. Give leadership something they can use.

You say it to your team. You hear yourself saying it. You know exactly what you mean.

Make it look less bad.

Good managers get bent that way. Not because they’re weak. Because they’re held to numbers they don’t control. They don’t control product quality, service capacity, pricing, customer fit, or half the decisions that created the mess. But they still own the forecast, the churn, the mood, the pace, and the fallout.

So they stop fixing causes and start managing how the damage sounds.

The customer sees the bill later. They get pushed to buy too early. They get promised more than the business can support. They hit implementation and find delay, confusion, gaps, and teams already at full stretch. What looked smooth in the sales cycle turns into backlog and apology on the other side.

Then the company asks the same questions. Why is churn up? Why is support drowning? Why does the forecast slip late? Why are good people worn out? Why does every quarter feel worse than the one before?

Because the numbers were cleaned up before the problem had a name. Because managers were told to keep things steady before the business made them solid. Because once that becomes normal, people stop asking whether something is true. They ask whether it is safe.

And once that happens, the business stops running on reality. It runs on whatever can get through the next meeting without causing trouble.
