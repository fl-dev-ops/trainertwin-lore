Gary Thomas is right about something important here. Britain’s industrial decline didn’t start with Margaret Thatcher. Mines, steelworks, shipyards and factories were already shedding jobs before 1979, and it carried on under governments of both parties afterwards. So “Thatcher destroyed British industry” is too easy.

But that doesn’t mean 1979 doesn’t matter. Thatcher didn’t start the decline, but her governments did change the rules of the economy. Union law changed. Major industries were privatised. Exchange controls went. Finance was deregulated. Tax changed. Capital became much freer to move.

Some of that worked. Manufacturing productivity rose. Strikes fell. Britain attracted money from around the world and built one of the strongest finance and services sectors anywhere. That makes the simple anti-Thatcher story hard to defend.

Then you have to ask the question from the other side. Germany, Italy, Japan and South Korea faced many of the same pressures we did: automation, global competition, cheaper production elsewhere and fewer workers needed to make more stuff. They lost manufacturing jobs too, but they kept much more of their industry.

France makes the story even messier. It kept much more state involvement and still lost a lot of industry. So I’m starting to think we’re arguing about the wrong thing.
The question isn’t who destroyed British industry. It’s what happened after old industries stopped working. What replaced them?

Britain became very good at finance and professional services. We kept real strengths in aerospace, pharmaceuticals and other high-value industries. So I don’t buy the idea that every lost factory was a failure, or that manufacturing is somehow better than services.

But Britain has also spent years investing less than many of the countries we compete with. That leaves a question I’d like answered. When an old mine, factory or industry disappeared, how good were we at creating the next thing?

What made people want to put long-term money into building businesses, factories, labs, machines, skills and infrastructure here? What made them put it somewhere else? And why did countries facing many of the same pressures get different results?

That brings me back to Gary’s strongest line: “You rebuild it by creating conditions in which somebody is prepared to open the next one.” Exactly. So let’s find out what those conditions really are.

Tax may be one. Energy may be another. But so might skills, finance, infrastructure, planning, trade, stable policy and what investors can earn elsewhere. Follow the evidence and see which ones actually changed the decision.

That seems a lot more useful than another argument about whether Thatcher saved Britain or wrecked it. Both stories are too simple, and simple stories are a lousy place to start making the next decision.
