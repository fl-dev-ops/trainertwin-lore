More capital does not automatically produce more progress. That is the uncomfortable point underneath Klisman Murati’s piece, and it should matter to anyone involved in capital allocation, portfolio performance, value creation, or board governance.

The easy answer is to blame greed, SaaS, AI hype, crowded trades, or short fund cycles. Some of that is true, but I think there is a deeper operating problem.

Capital keeps funding stories it can price before it has enough evidence it can trust.

That problem gets harder as the time horizon extends. Software can usually produce short-cycle signals: pipeline, ARR, usage, retention, expansion, and margin path. Even then, those signals can be misread.

Climate, infrastructure, biotech, resilience, advanced energy, and nation-scale projects work differently. The evidence takes longer to emerge. The dependencies are messier. The path is less linear.

That does not justify weaker standards. It requires better ones.

The real question is not only, “Can this generate a return?” It is, “What evidence makes this value realisable?” A surprising amount of capital allocation starts to wobble at that point.

By the time a board pack looks clean, the underlying assumption may already be socially protected. The model feels coherent. The narrative travels well. The risks have been acknowledged. People stop testing the assumption because too many decisions now depend on it remaining true.

That is usually the moment danger increases.

Not because people are dishonest. Most are acting in good faith. The problem is that pressure lowers evidence standards long before anyone notices.
You see it later as trapped capital, delayed exits, endless rescue plans, management fatigue, emergency refinancings, or years spent defending a decision that originally looked rational.

Before trusting a value story, I would test what is being treated as true, what evidence actually supports it, what time horizon the proof requires, who carries the consequence if the assumption fails, and what cost appears if capital moves too early.

That is not anti-growth. It is how serious capital protects itself from mistaking polished confidence for earned progress.

Capital does not punish the truth. It prices uncertainty.

The damage starts when uncertainty gets converted into confidence before the decision is made.

For me, that is the heart of Commercial Reality Governance: separating value that is realisable from value that is simply well presented.

Which signal gets over-trusted most often: market size, scalability, founder conviction, ARR, AI narrative, impact language, or board-pack confidence?
