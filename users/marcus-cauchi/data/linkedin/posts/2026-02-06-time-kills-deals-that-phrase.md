"Time kills deals."

That phrase is popular with leaders who confuse pressure with progress.

Time does not kill good deals.
Bad intervention does.

Most deals don't stall because sellers are slow.
They stall because the buyer is not yet able to decide.

Inside the buyer organisation, things are still unresolved.

Consensus is incomplete.
Risk is still moving sideways.
Someone who will carry the cost after signature has not agreed yet.

None of that responds to urgency.

So when pressure gets applied from the seller side, it doesn't accelerate decision.
It distorts it.

Forecasts get protected.
Signals get reinterpreted.
Buyer hesitation gets labelled as resistance instead of reality.

What gets called urgency is often anxiety with authority.

No one says the deal is not ready.
No one names that the buyer has not crossed their internal line yet.
No one marks the point where waiting stops being neutral and starts being necessary.

Instead, momentum is forced.

On the buyer side, that pressure shows up as caution.
More stakeholders.
More legal review.
Procurement as defence, not process.

Sometimes the deal dies.
Sometimes it closes and comes back as churn, discounting, or regret.

Then leadership blames time.
Or execution.
Or the rep.

But time did not kill the deal.
Intervention did.

Strong deals do not need to be pushed.
They need to be protected from pressure that mistakes uncertainty for delay.

Most deals are not lost because people wait.
They are lost because one side pretends the other side is simpler than it is.

If this feels uncomfortable, sit with it.

Which deal looks alive right now
only because no one can afford to stop it?

And be honest.
Who does that pressure actually serve.
The customer, or the system watching the numbers?
