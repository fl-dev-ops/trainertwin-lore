A bad decision rarely starts with someone being stupid. It usually starts smaller: a buyer asks for pricing and we hear intent, a deal moves stage and we hear progress, a sponsor sounds keen and we hear support, a customer goes quiet and we hear safety.

Then we act as if the story is true. We forecast it, discount, hire, promise delivery, brief the board, and only later discover that the evidence was thinner than the confidence built on top of it.

The damage starts when a signal becomes a claim, and the claim becomes confidence before it has earned it. That is why important decisions need better questions, not louder opinions.

What is the claim? What is the evidence? Where did it come from? How reliable is it? What are we assuming? What could change it? What else could be true? How confident should we really be?

Evidence is not the same as information. Agreement is not the same as truth. Confidence is not proof, and a good outcome does not prove the decision was sound.

A bad outcome does not prove the decision was stupid either. The real test is tougher: would the judgement still stand up if reality arrived early, loudly, and with consequences?

Better decisions come from a simple discipline: see the context, test the evidence, name the risk, make the call, check what happened, learn, and update the way you decide next time. 

That is how judgement gets stronger every time. Before the next forecast, renewal review, hiring call or board update, ask one question:

What are we about to trust that has not earned it yet?

That is the first brick in your Evidence Room.
