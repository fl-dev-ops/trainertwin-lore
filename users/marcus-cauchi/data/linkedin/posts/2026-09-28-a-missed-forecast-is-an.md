A missed forecast is an observation, not a diagnosis.

It tells you that what you expected to happen didn’t happen. It doesn’t tell you why.
Take a £500k deal in Commit. The customer wants to go ahead. The commercial case is agreed. One internal approval is still outstanding, but based on what the sales team knows, Commit looks reasonable.

This isn’t the only deal in Commit. Together, those deals form part of the quarter’s forecast, and the business has brought two planned hires forward partly on the strength of it.

Now the deal slips.
Perhaps that approval usually takes six weeks and nobody found that out before calling the deal Commit. Maybe something genuinely changed at the customer after the call was made.
Or new evidence came to light that things were changing, but it never reached the people making the forecast.

All three end with the same missed number. They don’t have the same cause, and they don’t have the same fix. Tighter inspection of the number won’t address a failure that entered somewhere else. So before changing the forecast process, ask what actually happened.
What did we know when we made the call?
What changed?
When did we know?
Did the forecast change when the evidence changed?

Forecast accuracy matters. But you can make a poor call and get the outcome right. You can make a reasonable call and get the outcome wrong. If the deal slips and the hires have already been brought forward, “the forecast was wrong” isn’t a diagnosis.

Find out whether there was a preventable error, and where it first entered.
