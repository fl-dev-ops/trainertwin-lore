Your CRM says you have £4m in Commit this quarter.

Now look at what happened to genuinely comparable Commit at the same point in previous quarters.

Suppose, for the sake of the example, that 75% of that value closed in the quarter.

That doesn’t mean this £4m will turn into £3m. The deals won’t be identical. Conditions may have changed. One large deal could change the result completely. But ignoring the history doesn’t make the £4m more likely to arrive either. It’s evidence, and it belongs in the picture.
Here’s where the problem starts.

Sales may have perfectly good reasons for putting each deal where it is. The trouble begins when those individual judgements are added together and other people treat the total as more certain than it is.

On a report, £4m in Commit can easily be read as £4m the business expects to receive.
If people outside sales don’t know what normally happens to deals carrying that label, £4m of sales judgement can become £4m of expected revenue somewhere else. Delivery may reserve capacity. Finance may commit spend against it.

Cash is the sharpest case. Good evidence that a customer will buy isn’t the same as knowing when they’ll sign, when you can invoice, and when the money will arrive.
None of those decisions is necessarily wrong. The risk is that they rest on more confidence than the evidence supports.

You still need to know what’s behind the number. What has actually been established with the customer? What still has to happen? Which deals depend on one unresolved event? Has anything changed since they went into Commit?

So don’t just ask whether Sales will hit £4m.
Ask who else is relying on it.

What does your business do with the Commit number?
