The day after I left my former franchise in 2020, my name disappeared from the cover of a book I’d co-authored.

Dave Davies and I wrote Making Channel Sales Work. A book about building successful commercial partnerships.

The irony wasn’t lost on me.

But there’s another side to the story. My relationship with the master franchise had been fractious almost from the start. We’d worked together for 16 years, and I certainly contributed to the difficulties between us.

I wasn’t an innocent bystander.

I don’t know what reasoning lay behind the decision to remove my name, and I’m sure they’d have their own account of what happened. But leaving didn’t change who wrote the book.
And it raises a question that’s stuck with me.

What does it actually mean to be partnerable?

We spend a lot of time asking whether someone would make a good partner for us. How often do we ask whether we’d make a good partner for them? We’re asking people to put a great deal at risk. Their time, money, knowledge, relationships and reputation.

They need good reasons to believe we can deliver what we promise, that the relationship will be worthwhile for both sides, and that we’ll treat them fairly when things don’t go to plan.
Especially when our interests diverge.

Can we disagree without diminishing one another?
Can we recognise what someone contributed, even when we no longer want to work together?
Can we acknowledge our own part when things go wrong?
And can we recognise when a partnership needs to change, or end?

I write about my own part in another broken relationship in my book, Partnerable.

Not because I have all the answers, but because understanding what we contribute to a relationship matters just as much as understanding what the other person does.
You can stop being someone’s partner without ceasing to be partnerable.
That’s part of what Partnerable: How to Build Trusted Partnerships Others Would Hate to be Without available now on Amazon, explores. (Links in first comment)

Not just how to choose better partners, but how to become someone others have good reasons to choose, invest in and work with. And how to recognise when partnering isn’t the right decision.

When a partnership goes wrong, are we as willing to examine our own contribution as we are to question the other person’s?
