Most companies don’t miss the number. They hit it, then spend the next year dealing with what came with it. Discounts that don’t come back. Customers who were never going to stick. Pipeline that looked solid right up until it wasn’t. 
Renewals everyone assumed were fine.

You don’t feel it in the quarter. You feel it later. Churn creeps up. Margins thin out. CS is always firefighting. Forecasts need more explaining than they should. Someone eventually says, “We’re growing, but this feels harder than it should.”

Here’s the part people don’t love admitting. Sales usually doesn’t fail at closing. It fails earlier, at choosing what to close. Wrong markets. Urgency that sounded real at the time. Deals pushed through because the quarter needed them. Structure left vague until procurement showed up. Logos valued more than whether the customer would actually make it work.

The cost shows up everywhere else. Finance pays in forecasts no one fully trusts. CS pays in burnout. Product pays in noise. Leadership pays in credibility. Investors pay in valuation.

At some point, trying to sell better stops helping. What helps is asking better questions early, before the deal exists.
Should we even be selling here?
Is this opportunity real, or just loud?
If we sign this, does it actually hold together?
And once it’s live, will this customer renew and grow?

When you ask those questions early, sales doesn’t get faster. It gets calmer. 

Fewer deals, better ones. Less discounting. Higher order value. Renewals that don’t turn into renegotiations. Expansion that comes from use, not pressure.

It slows down deals that are moving too fast for the wrong reasons. It stops deals that would’ve caused damage later. It protects the ones worth protecting.
Revenue stops feeling random. It starts feeling owned.

That isn’t sales ops. It’s governance. It’s revenue you can plan around.
If this feels familiar, the fix isn’t motivational. It’s structural. There’s a way to catch it before it shows up in year two.
