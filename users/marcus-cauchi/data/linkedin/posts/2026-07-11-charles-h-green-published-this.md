Charles H. Green published this excellent series. Highly recommended.

The thought I keep coming back to across the whole series is that fear can distort judgement and still carry useful information. Sometimes it's ego, nerves or old baggage. Sometimes it's the first honest sign that the seller doesn't know enough, the offer doesn't fit, the numbers are too optimistic, or the buyer is being asked to take too much on trust.

That's where selling has to move from our side to theirs. The buyer is trying to work out whether the economics hold, whether the change will fit the business they already have, and whether they can make the decision and roll it out without creating a mess.

Behind each of those sit practical, political and personal concerns. Will it work in the real world? Who has to support it or give something up? Who is left exposed if it goes wrong?

That's why hesitation is not always something to overcome. Quite often it is a sign that part of the decision is unfinished, and that the buyer is doing their job properly.

The seller’s job is to help finish that work. Test the numbers, surface the awkward dependencies, involve the people who will inherit the consequences, and agree what would make either side stop or change course.

There is also a duty on management. You can't ask sellers to be honest about risk, then punish them for bringing unwelcome evidence into a forecast call. If managers reward confidence before buyer evidence improves, fear-driven behaviour becomes entirely predictable.

The conclusion I take from the series is that good sellers don't become fearless. They learn to tell the difference between fear that needs challenging and fear that is warning everyone about something real, then help the buyer make a decision neither side has to regret.

Read Charlie’s full series, then ask yourself this: when fear shows up in a deal, do you treat it as something to overcome, or as evidence that part of the decision still needs work? The answer may tell you whether your sales culture builds trust or merely rewards confidence.
