A partner programme can hit its recruitment target and still be in serious trouble.

I've seen $billion vendors recruit hundreds of partners, celebrate the number and then discover that only half of them ever became active. When it costs more than £15,000 to recruit each one, that is not a soft problem. It is a large, expensive hole in the operating plan.

The warning signs are usually there. A small number of partner managers are doing most of the work. They are chasing updates, finding specialists, rescuing deals and trying to explain why signed partners are not producing what the forecast promised.

On paper, the programme is growing. In practice, the company has bought a list of names and mistaken it for capacity.

Signing a partner proves very little. It shows that both sides were willing to begin. It does not show that the partner can find the right customers, explain the offer, protect their margin, get support when they need it or deliver the result without someone inside the vendor stepping in.

This is where many partner programmes go wrong. They measure recruitment because recruitment is visible. They avoid measuring reliance because reliance exposes whether the operating model actually works.

The useful question is not how many partners have signed?

The useful question is how many could still create good revenue if your best partner manager stopped rescuing them?
