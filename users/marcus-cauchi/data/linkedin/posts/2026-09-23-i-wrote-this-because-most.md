I wrote this because most CRMs tell us far more about what the seller has done than about what’s happening on the customer’s side.

I still agree with it. But I’ve spent a lot of time since then looking at pipeline, forecasts and the decisions companies make from them, and I think I stopped too soon. Even if your CRM gives you a good picture of the buying process, there’s another question. What is everyone else in the business entitled to assume because a deal is in the forecast?

Take a £500k deal in Commit. The customer wants to proceed, the commercial case is agreed and there’s an internal approval still to get through. The seller knows that, the manager knows that, and they’ve decided there’s enough evidence to call it Commit. Fair enough.

Now suppose Finance hires two people because that £500k is expected this quarter. Nothing has changed at the customer and no new evidence has appeared. But the company has made a second decision on the back of the first. If the deal slips now, the business misses the number and is also paying two salaries it might not otherwise have taken on yet.

That can happen even when the CRM is accurate and nobody is stuffing the forecast.

The seller heard the customer. They know what the customer actually said, what they took from it and where they’re still making a judgement. They probably know which parts of the deal bother them, even if they still believe it will close. Then all of that has to become a record someone else can use: a stage, a forecast category, a close date, some notes, maybe a probability, a score or an AI summary.

The next person sees £500k in Commit. They may not know that the final approval hasn’t happened, how confident the customer sounded when they discussed it, whether the seller has direct evidence or is joining the dots, or what would have to happen for the deal to slip. Some of the confidence can survive while some of the uncertainty gets lost.

AI could help here if it keeps track of where the evidence came from, separates what the customer said from what we’ve inferred and leaves the unknowns visible. But if we feed it a record where those things have already been blurred together, I’m not sure a better summary helps. We may just get a cleaner, more convincing version of the same judgement.

So what I’d add to the original article is a second question: what will people elsewhere in the company do because they believe what the CRM tells them?

A deal can be worth pursuing without being safe to hire against. It can deserve a place in the forecast without every decision that follows deserving the same confidence.
***
For the CRM and RevOps people here, when a deal becomes Commit in your business, what exactly have you decided everyone else can safely rely on?
