The same problem keeps coming back.

Sales is missing its numbers. Customer success is struggling with adoption. Managers are spending too much time sorting out problems their people ought to be able to handle.

So the business responds.
More training. More meetings. Better reporting. Someone senior gets involved to make sure things happen. Sometimes that’s exactly what’s needed.

But what if we’re asking people to work harder at solving the wrong problem?

Think about the last time someone senior stepped in to rescue a difficult situation. They may have saved an important customer, protected revenue or prevented a costly mistake.

What happened afterwards?
Did the people involved become better equipped to handle the next one, or did they learn that difficult decisions get passed upstairs?

The same question applies elsewhere.
A salesperson can win a contract without establishing whether the customer is ready to use what they’ve bought. Marketing can bring in more enquiries without knowing whether they’re likely to become worthwhile customers. A business can retain an account without understanding how much value the customer is getting.

None of those outcomes automatically means someone has done a poor job.
But the consequences often turn up somewhere else, long after the original decision was made. And the people dealing with them are usually expected to work harder.

Before your next performance review, choose one problem that keeps coming back.
Find out what’s actually happening, rather than starting with the explanation you’ve been given.

What's already been decided? What are people still assuming? Who can change it, and what would justify doing so?

You may discover that the existing approach is sound. In that case, leave it alone.
You may find that something important has changed, or that a decision everyone thought had been made never was.
Either way, you’ve got a better basis for deciding what to do next.

Because making heroics unnecessary doesn’t mean people never have to do difficult things.
It means we stop making difficult things harder than they need to be.
