The evidence pack was immaculate.

Every change was logged. Every version could be restored. Every test result had a timestamp.

Then the paper reached the person whose name would sit beneath the number.

They didn't ask what had changed. They asked who had authorised the system to change itself.

Brad Wolfe has identified the gap between technical control and accountable authority. Logs can prove what happened. Tests can show whether it worked. Neither proves that the change was permitted, that the system stayed within its mandate, or that the person accepting the risk had been given a meaningful approval point.

Self-improving agents do not make governance impossible. They make the placement of authority part of the architecture.

A named person must approve what the system may change, the evidence it may use, the consequences it may create, and the conditions that force it to stop. Changes may proceed automatically only while they remain inside that authorised envelope and continue to pass its tests.

Crossing the envelope is not another automated improvement. It is a new decision requiring fresh authority.

Many organisations will miss this. They will build systems that produce excellent evidence about changes no accountable person had the power to accept or refuse.

The system may have behaved exactly as designed.

The harder question is whether anyone had authorised that design for the consequence it produced.
