The opportunity appears in CRM and everyone behaves as though the race has just started.

It hasn’t.

By then, the buyer may already have named the problem, asked around, worked out who sounds credible, ruled some options out and built a view of what “safe” looks like. They may even have decided what evidence they’ll believe before a salesperson ever gets near the conversation.

Most GTM teams don’t arrive too late because sellers are lazy. They arrive too late because the system tells them the buyer is “in play” after the buyer has already done the hard thinking.

That’s the trap.

Most GTM is built around what the seller can see: leads, meetings, MQLs, intent spikes, stage movement, proposals, next steps and forecast calls. Useful stuff, but late stuff. It shows seller activity better than buyer reality.

CRM doesn’t show when the buyer first started worrying. It’s blind to who shaped the problem before sales arrived. It fails to show what got ruled out before the vendor list was made. It won’t tell you what evidence the buyer trusts, who bears the risk if this goes wrong, or what has already become politically unsafe.

That’s where teams get fooled.

They see movement and call it demand. They see interest and call it intent. A friendly champion becomes “strong buyer engagement.” A next step becomes “progress.” A forecast becomes a fact. Then everyone acts shocked when the deal stalls, slips, shrinks or disappears.

So yes, spot buying movement earlier. But don’t kid yourself that every signal is meaningful.

A funding round might matter. A new CFO might matter. A website visit, job ad or intent spike might matter. Or it might be noise wearing a nice jacket.

The question isn’t “did something happen?”

The question is “does this change what the buyer is likely to do next?”

That’s the job.

Find the movement before it becomes pipeline. Then test whether the movement is real, weak, noisy or wishful thinking.

Because more pipeline won’t save you if you’re late to the decision.
