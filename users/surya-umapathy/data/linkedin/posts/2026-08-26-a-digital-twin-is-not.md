A digital twin is not a copied mind. It is a bounded, testable model of how someone is likely to respond in specific situations.

But to test whether we had modelled a trainer, we first had to model the learners who brought out those choices.

At Trainer Twin (Forever Learning), we manually studied 22 trainer-led sessions—over ten hours of conversation. We did not just collect questions or catchphrases. 

We broke each interaction into three parts:
👉🏼 Observation - What happened so far? What did the learner understand, miss, exaggerate or contradict?
👉🏼 Decision - Should the trainer probe, challenge, ask for evidence, offer a hint, teach or move on?
👉🏼 Behaviour - How did the trainer express that decision through acknowledgement, tone and phrasing?

One pattern stood out - The agenda was fixed, but the depth was adaptive.

A familiar opening question could lead to different conversations depending on the learner’s answer.

This gave us a testable question: "Given the same observation, can the twin choose a similar next move and express it recognisably?"

The original sessions helped us understand the trainer, but only contained the learners who participated. They could not show how the trainer would respond to every learner type.

So we created a learner behaviour matrix. We varied
• technical depth,
• communication clarity,
• ownership,
• confidence,
• response to pressure,
• ability to self-correct.

That produced learners who were
• articulate but shallow,
• hands-on but inarticulate,
• honest about limited ownership,
• inflated,
• evasive,
• coachable and self-correcting.

We used DeepEval’s Conversation Simulator to place these learners in different contexts and interact with the trainer model, that gave us more than additional conversations: "A behavioural test environment."

We could now test whether the trainer challenged confident but shallow language, helped someone capable but inarticulate, detected inflated ownership, or adapted when a learner corrected themselves.

The simulations showed that a fluent conversation could still contain the wrong decision. A “conversation quality” score was not enough.

We separated:
👉🏼 Learner fidelity — Did the learner stay true to its persona?
👉🏼 Decision fidelity — Did the twin choose a trainer-like move?
👉🏼 Behaviour fidelity — Did it express that move like the trainer?
👉🏼 Conversation quality — Was the session grounded, adaptive and useful?


Automated checks helped with consistency and rules. Real-session comparison and trainer review helped evaluate fidelity.

Where we landed:
We did not just synthesize training data. We created a repeatable way to observe, challenge and improve a bounded model of trainer behaviour.


My biggest takeaway:
To model an expert, do not study only the expert. Model the people and situations that cause their judgment to appear.
