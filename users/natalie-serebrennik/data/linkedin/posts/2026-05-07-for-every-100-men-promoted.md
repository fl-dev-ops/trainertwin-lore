For every 100 men promoted to manager, 93 women are. 

And for women of color, that number drops to 74.

This data is from the 2025 McKinsey and LeanIn Women in the Workplace report - the largest study of its kind, and it hasn’t meaningfully changed in 11 years.

Most companies respond to this by investing in one-off events, mentorship initiatives, or confidence workshops. 

Sponsorship commitments that look good in an annual report but move the number by almost nothing.

All because they're solving for the wrong problem.

The broken rung isn't a pipeline problem nor is it a lack of confidence in women compared to men. 

It isn't even a bias problem at the individual level, though that exists too.

It's a visibility and advocacy problem that compounds at every decision point and is almost entirely invisible to the people making the decisions.

Promotions are not awarded to the most qualified candidate. They’re awarded to the candidate whose qualifications are most legible to the decision-makers in the room. 

And when the systems that create legibility (informal sponsorship, access to high-visibility work, inclusion in the conversations that happen before the formal process begins) are unevenly distributed, the outcome is uneven. 

Every time.

The companies making real progress on this are auditing their promotion process for where advocacy breaks down. 

They're looking at who gets access to stretch assignments and who gets passed over for them. 

They're asking managers directly: who on your team are you actively sponsoring - not mentoring, but actually sponsoring?

And what have you done about it in the last 90 days?

That's a different conversation than "we need more women in leadership." 

It's a conversation about process, accountability, and where the real work actually lives.

What would your company find if it audited the last promotion cycle for where advocacy was present, and where it was absent?
