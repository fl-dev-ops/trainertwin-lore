Giving feedback isn't the hard part.

Getting someone to actually do something about it is.

One of my clients was getting very frustrated with a direct report.

She'd raised the same performance issue multiple times, but nothing was changing.

And she couldn't understand why.

When we walked through how she'd been delivering the feedback, we spotted the problem.

She was so worried about hurting her employee's feelings that she wasn't being clear about what actually needed to change.

And I get it.

When you genuinely care about the people on your team, the last thing you want is to discourage them or damage the relationship you've worked hard to build.

But there's a big difference between being considerate of someone's feelings and avoiding an uncomfortable conversation.

You can spend 20 minutes explaining a performance issue, soften it with a few compliments, and walk away thinking you've made your point.

Meanwhile, your employee walks away thinking everything is pretty much fine.

And then you're frustrated when the same issue comes up again.

But how can you expect someone to improve if they don't fully understand what's wrong in the first place?

So with my client, we focused on four things she needed to communicate more clearly:

1️⃣ What's the actual problem? Be specific about the behaviour or performance issue, with real examples.

2️⃣ Why does it matter? Explain how it's affecting the team, the business, or the people relying on them.

3️⃣ What needs to change? Make sure they understand exactly what you expect them to do differently.

4️⃣ What happens if it doesn't? Be upfront about the consequences if the issue continues.

And most important, give the person an opportunity to respond. There may be something going on that you haven't considered.

You don't need to be a jerk to hold someone accountable.

You can be empathetic, supportive, and genuinely invested in someone's success while still being very clear about what isn't working.

In fact, I'd argue you owe them that clarity.
