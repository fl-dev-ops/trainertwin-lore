Nothing destroys trust in a leader like an ego that can't handle being wrong.

I've seen firsthand how damaging it can be to work for a leader who refuses to take accountability.

You know the type.

When things go well, they're quick to take credit.

When things go wrong, suddenly everyone else is responsible.

They encourage people to speak up, but the moment someone challenges their thinking, they get defensive.

And if they make a bad decision? Good luck getting them to admit it.

These are some of the narcissistic leadership behaviours that make working for someone absolutely exhausting.

But what concerns me most isn't necessarily the behaviour itself.

It's how everyone around that leader starts adjusting to accommodate it.

You stop challenging decisions because you know how your manager will react.

You become selective about what information you share.

They spend more time figuring out how to deliver bad news without upsetting your boss than actually addressing the problem.

And eventually, you've got an entire team that's more concerned with managing their leader's ego than doing their best work.

Think about how much that costs an organization.

Problems that could have been addressed early get ignored.

Good ideas never make it into the conversation.

And talented people start looking for opportunities elsewhere.

All because one person can't handle being wrong.

I've always had far more respect for leaders who can simply say:

"I got that wrong. Looking back, I should have handled it differently. Here's what I'm going to do about it."

I'm sure it won't surprise you to hear how often this comes up in my coaching conversations with clients.

And if I had the chance to speak directly to their managers, here's exactly what I’d say:

You don't need to be the smartest person in every room. You don't need to have every answer. And you're certainly not going to make the right decision every single time.

But you do need to create an environment where people feel comfortable telling you when something isn't working, even when you're the reason it isn't working.

If your team is afraid to tell you you've made a mistake, you've got a much bigger leadership problem than the mistake itself.

What would you say to a leader like this?
