He believed asking for feedback would make him look insecure.

Like it would signal doubt.
Or worse, confirm a fear he didn’t want to face.

So he did what most high performers do instead.

He focused on results and assumed they’d speak for themselves.

They didn’t.

Not when peers were being pulled into stretch projects.

Not when leadership felt more connected to people who weren’t more capable… just more aligned.

That’s when he came to me.

The real issue wasn’t confidence or performance.

It was this belief:
Feedback felt like a threat instead of a tool.

So he avoided it.

What he didn’t see was the cost of staying in the dark.

No clear expectations.

No shared definition of what “doing well” actually meant.

No signal to leadership that growth genuinely mattered to him.

But here’s the thing:

Avoiding feedback doesn’t protect you.
It just keeps you uninformed.

Feedback exists whether it’s spoken or not.

And the sooner you understand it, the sooner you can act on it.

The hard part wasn’t the conversation.
It was committing to have it.

Once he did, the path forward was straightforward.

This is how real growth happens.

Not in bold moves.
But in quiet decisions to face the thing you’ve been avoiding.

If this hit a little too close to home, that’s usually the signal.
