63% of employee exits in 2025 were preventable.

Not because the person wasn't fairly compensated. 

Not because the role was wrong. 

Because nobody caught the signals early enough to do anything about them.

This is the retention problem most organizations are not solving - because they're measuring it wrong. 

They track exits. They conduct off-boarding surveys. 

They review the data after the person has already accepted another offer. 

And then they make changes for the next person, not for the one who just walked out the door.

By the time someone is in an exit interview, the decision is 6-12 months old. 

The survey is a formality. 

The real conversation happened inside that person's head long before anyone in leadership knew there was a problem.

The signals that predict exit are almost never about the work. 

They're about whether the person believes they have a future in their company, and whether anyone senior has made that case to them recently.

Disengagement looks like: a high performer who stops raising their hand. 

A reliable contributor who gets quieter in meetings. 

Someone who used to push back and now just agrees. 

None of these show up in every day performance metrics. 

But all of them are visible to a manager who knows what they're looking for.

The first thing a leader can do right now is look at their highest performers. 

Not the ones at risk of underperforming, the ones they absolutely cannot afford to lose.

And ask honestly: when did I last have a direct conversation with this person about their future here? 

Not a performance check-in. A conversation about their future.

If the answer is "I'm not sure" or "not recently," that's a leadership gap.

What would it cost your organization if the person you can least afford to lose has already decided to leave, and you just don’t know it yet?
