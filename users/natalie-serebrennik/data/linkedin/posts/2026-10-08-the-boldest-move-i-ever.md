The boldest move I ever made in my career is also the one I regret waiting so long to make.

Years into my career, I discovered I was underpaid compared to my peers.

So I raised it with my manager and was told, “Next year, we'll fix it.”

And I believed him.

Then next year came and nothing changed.

More conversations. More reassurance. More waiting.

For years, I trusted that someone else would eventually fix a problem I had already made clear.

It wasn't until I returned from my second maternity leave, under a new manager, that I decided I was done waiting.

I told her: “I'm not okay with this and I'm taking it up the chain.”

And I did. One senior leader at a time.

But I didn't walk into those conversations and immediately start talking about how underpaid I was.

I started with my value.
The results I'd delivered.
The revenue I'd generated.
The responsibilities I'd taken on.
The impact I was having on the business.

I built the business case first.

Then I showed them the disconnect between that contribution and how I was being compensated. And I asked them to fix it.

By the end of that year, I added an additional 6-figures to my bank account.

The biggest lesson wasn't that advocating for myself worked.

It was realizing how many years I'd spent waiting for someone else to do it for me.

My manager knew I was unhappy.

I'd raised the issue.
I'd been promised change.

And still, nothing happened until I changed how I was advocating for myself.

That's the part I wish I'd understood sooner.

Self-advocacy isn't just telling people you deserve more.

It's making the business case for your value so clearly that the people making the decision understand exactly what you're asking for and why.

If you've been waiting for someone else to notice the gap between what you're contributing and what you're getting in return, don't assume another year of great work will automatically fix it.

At some point, you have to make the case.
