I sabotaged my own career growth with one addiction.

I was addicted to being the person everyone could count on.

The one who always said yes, took on more responsibility, stayed late, and somehow managed to get everything done.

And the more people relied on me, the more convinced I became that I was doing exactly what it took to get ahead.

I genuinely believed that the harder I worked, the faster I'd get promoted.

Except that wasn't happening.

And whenever someone asked why I wasn't spending more time on my own development, I had the same answer.

I don't have time.

I remember an early mentor telling me something I'll never forget:

"Rookie leaders make excuses. Great leaders make time."

That one stung.

Because as much as I hated hearing it, I knew there was truth to it.

I was working my butt off, but I wasn't necessarily working on the things that would help me get ahead.

I was so focused on being great at my current job that I wasn't making enough time to prepare for the next one.

And the most frustrating part, I genuinely thought I was doing everything right.

I was delivering results, exceeding expectations, and making myself indispensable.

But I wasn't spending nearly enough time building relationships across the business, developing the skills I'd need at the next level, or making sure the right people understood where I wanted to go.

I was prioritizing everyone else's needs over my own career growth.

And once I realized that, I knew something had to change.

I started being more selective about the meetings I attended.

I delegated work that didn't actually need me.

And I began blocking time in my calendar specifically for my own development.

Not to catch up on emails. Not to squeeze in another project.

But to actually work on the things that would help ME advance.

Because if you don't make the time to work on yourself, your day-to-day responsibilities will happily consume every minute you have.

There will always be another deadline, another meeting, another problem that needs your attention.

And if you're someone who takes pride in getting sh*t done, it's incredibly easy to convince yourself that all of it is important.

But being busy and making progress in your career aren't the same thing.

So here's something worth asking yourself:

When you look at your calendar this week, how much time have you actually set aside for your own career growth?

Not your team's priorities. Not your boss's priorities.

Yours.
