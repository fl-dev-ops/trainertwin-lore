Executives keep promoting top talent into leadership for the wrong reasons.

And it’s hurting more people than anyone talks about.

We spend so much time defining what makes a great leader:

→ Caring about their people
→ Encouraging growth
→ Supporting success

Yet somehow, the wrong people keep getting promoted.

Because excelling at the work and wanting to lead people are two very different things.

That assumption costs companies money, morale, and retention.

But it also costs the individual something even bigger:

Their joy.
Their confidence.
Their sense of alignment.

Because deep down, you’re saying yes to a path that was never yours.

I’ve seen this play out more times than I can count.

It’s really hard to turn down a promotion because:

→ You don’t want to look ungrateful
→ You don’t want to seem “less ambitious”
→ You don’t want to stall your career

So you say yes.

Choosing to disappoint yourself rather than them.

And then months later, you wake up in a role you never asked for, carrying expectations you never agreed to, and wondering why you feel disconnected from work you used to love.

Not everyone wants to lead.
And not everyone should.

Good leadership requires a specific mindset, a specific emotional capacity, and a genuine desire to develop people.

Being exceptional at your job doesn’t automatically mean you’ll enjoy managing others.

So here’s a better question we should be asking high performers:

What does growth actually look like for you?

Maybe it has nothing to do with leading others and everything to do with:

→ Deepening your expertise
→ Expanding your influence
→ Owning bigger outcomes
→ Driving meaningful results in new ways

It’s time to stop defaulting to a promote-to-lead model.

Growth can look like many things.

And if we’re serious about retaining our best people, we have to stop forcing them into one path.

Agree?
