The resignation is never the moment it starts.

By the time a high performer hands in their notice, the decision has usually been made for weeks. 

Sometimes months. 

And during that entire window, they showed up, delivered, said nothing - while quietly building their exit.

Management almost always describes it the same way afterward: we had no idea.

But the signals were there:

They stopped volunteering for things they used to raise their hand for. 

They got quieter in meetings where they used to drive the conversation. 

They stopped asking for feedback, when growth was their #1 priority just a few months back.

Performance never dipped, but they weren’t present in the way they used to be.

The gap between a high performer checking out and a high performer walking out is where the most preventable turnover lives.

And most companies have no system for catching it.

The managers who retain their best people aren't doing anything sophisticated either.

They're simply honoring the human element in leadership.

The part that requires showing up for your people before they stop showing up for you.

Like:

Having real conversations early enough to matter. 

Asking what their people need before the need becomes a reason to leave. 

Treating retention as an ongoing practice, not a crisis response.

And when I was asked recently why most don’t do this, one thing came to mind immediately:

Because no one taught them to look for it before it was too late.

When did you last have a conversation with your highest performer that wasn't about the work?
