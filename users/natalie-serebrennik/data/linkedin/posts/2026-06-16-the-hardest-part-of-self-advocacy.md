The hardest part of self-advocacy isn't the conversation.

It's convincing yourself the conversation is necessary.

For years I told myself I didn't need to advocate for myself. 

That doing it well was enough. 

That making noise about my own contributions was the kind of thing other people did, people who needed to compensate for something.

I didn't see it as strategy. 

I saw it as unnecessary.

And that belief kept me quiet through some of the most important moments of my career.

When I finally accepted that advocacy was part of the job, the next question was harder than I expected.

What does this actually look like in practice?

Because there's a version of self-advocacy that feels like oversharing. 

Like you're performing your own highlight reel. 

Like every conversation becomes a pitch for why you're great.

That's not it.

Here's what it actually is:

Connecting your work to outcomes your organization cares about.

Not listing what you did, but naming what changed because of it.

Making your thinking visible before you're asked.

In meetings, in updates, in the conversations happening one level above you.

Having explicit conversations about where you want to go. 

Not hinting, not hoping someone connects the dots, but saying it directly to the people who can influence it.

None of that is bragging.

Because bragging is self-serving and advocacy is strategic.

The difference isn't what you say. It's why you say it and how you frame it.

Once I understood that distinction, the discomfort didn't disappear entirely.

But it stopped being a reason not to act.

What's the story you've been telling yourself about why advocating for yourself feels wrong?
